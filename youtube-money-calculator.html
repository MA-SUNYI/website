How the YouTube Money Calculator Works

Ad revenue on YouTube starts with CPM, the price advertisers pay per 1,000 ad impressions. That is not what you earn. Two things sit between advertiser spend and your bank account: YouTube keeps 45 percent of long-form watch page ad revenue, and a large share of your views never show a paid impression in the first place.

The calculator models that chain explicitly, so you can see where the money leaks instead of staring at one blended number:

Monetized views = Monthly views × monetized rate
Gross ad revenue = (Monetized views ÷ 1,000) × CPM
Your AdSense revenue = Gross ad revenue × 0.55
Effective RPM = Your revenue ÷ monthly views × 1,000

Worked example: a channel with 500,000 monthly views, 60 percent of them monetized, in a niche where advertisers pay a $10 CPM. Monetized views come to 300,000, gross ad revenue is 300 × $10 = $3,000, and your 55 percent share is $1,650 a month. Spread across all 500,000 views that is an effective RPM of $3.30 — the figure worth comparing against other creators' claims.

Change only the niche and the picture shifts hard. The same 500,000 views at a $4 CPM, typical for gaming and entertainment, produces $1,200 of gross revenue and $660 to the creator. Same channel, same work, 60 percent less money. That gap is the single most useful insight this tool produces, and it is why a view count on its own tells you almost nothing.

What Actually Moves the Number

Audience country. Advertiser demand tracks purchasing power. A US-heavy audience routinely pays three to five times more per impression than a mixed global audience on identical content.

Watch time and ad suitability. Longer videos support mid-rolls, and videos flagged as limited for advertisers simply earn less per impression. Both show up in your monetized playback rate, which is why that input deserves a real number rather than a guess.

Seasonality. Q4 advertising budgets push CPMs up, and January pulls them back down. A single strong month is not a new baseline, and annualising December revenue is a reliable way to disappoint yourself.

Format. Shorts monetise through a different mechanism than long-form, so mixing both into one view count produces an RPM that matches neither. Run them separately.

Eligibility, Payout Thresholds and Timing

None of the revenue above exists until a channel joins the YouTube Partner Program, which requires 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 valid public watch hours over the previous 12 months, with an equivalent Shorts-view path for short-form channels. Below that line the calculator is a planning exercise, not income, and it is worth knowing that the 4,000-hour gate is usually the harder of the two for new channels.

Once monetized, earnings accumulate in the linked AdSense account rather than arriving directly. AdSense pays monthly once the balance clears its minimum threshold, and US creators must submit tax details before the first payout. The practical consequence is a lag of roughly a month between the views that earned the money and the deposit, so December revenue lands in January. Anyone budgeting rent from channel income should plan for that delay rather than treating an estimate as same-month cash.

Video length also matters more than most creators expect. Videos past the eight-minute mark can carry manual mid-roll breaks, which adds advertising inventory to the same view. That raises revenue per view, but stuffing breaks into a video that cannot hold attention costs retention, and the resulting drop in watch time can offset the extra ad slot entirely.

Where These Estimates Break Down

The output is a projection, not a payout statement. If your channel is already monetized, open YouTube Studio and read your actual RPM instead — no published benchmark beats your own data. Two caveats matter most. First, a blended CPM from a blog post says nothing about your audience; use it only when you have no data at all. Second, estimated revenue is not cash: AdSense has a payment threshold, payouts lag the month they were earned, and self-employment tax takes a share that no calculator on this page accounts for.

Used carefully, the tool answers a narrow question well: what is this volume of views plausibly worth in this niche. It cannot tell you whether your next video will perform.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does YouTube pay per 1,000 views?

Most channels land between $1 and $10 per 1,000 total views after the platform share. Finance and software sit at the top of that range, gaming and music at the bottom, and audience country moves any of them by a factor of several.

Why is my real RPM lower than the CPM people quote online?

CPM is advertiser spend per 1,000 impressions before any split. You keep 55 percent of long-form ad revenue, and only part of your views are monetized at all. CPM × monetized rate × 0.55 is the honest conversion.

What monetized playback rate should I enter?

Between 40 and 80 percent is typical for long-form, with 60 percent a reasonable default. Ad blockers, non-monetized countries, limited-suitability videos and skipped pre-rolls all pull it lower.

Can I use this calculator for YouTube Shorts?

Not with the same inputs. Shorts pay out of a Creator Pool allocated after music licensing, and creators receive 45 percent of that allocation rather than 55 percent of ad revenue. Calculate Shorts separately.

How many views do I need to earn $1,000 a month?

About 303,000 monthly views at a $10 CPM and 60 percent monetized playback, with no other income streams. At a $4 CPM the same goal needs roughly 758,000 views.

Do sponsorships count toward my RPM?

No. Brand deals, memberships and affiliate commissions never appear in AdSense RPM. This calculator adds them on top and keeps each stream separate so you can see which one is actually carrying the channel.
