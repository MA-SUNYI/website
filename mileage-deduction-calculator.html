Mileage Deduction Calculator

If you drive for work and file a Schedule C, the car is usually one of your largest business expenses. There are two ways to write it off: multiply business miles by the IRS standard rate, or total what the vehicle actually cost you and deduct the business-use share. Most people pick the standard rate because it takes five minutes, but it is not always the bigger number. This calculator runs both methods on your own figures and shows which one leaves more on the table, plus the tax you actually save rather than just the income you subtract.

How the Two Methods Are Calculated

The standard mileage rate is a flat cents-per-mile figure the IRS publishes each year to stand in for every cost of running a car: fuel, insurance, depreciation, tyres, repairs and fees. The formula is as short as it gets.

Standard deduction = business miles x rate. Drive 12,000 business miles at the 2026 business rate of 0.725 and you deduct 8,700 dollars. Nothing else about the car enters the calculation, which is exactly why it is popular.

The actual expense method works the other way round. You add up every real cost for the year, then take only the business-use share.

Business use % = business miles / total miles. Actual deduction = total vehicle cost x business use %. Take 9,900 dollars of total costs (2,400 fuel, 1,800 insurance, 4,200 depreciation, 900 repairs, 600 fees) with 12,000 of 18,000 miles counted as business. The business share is 66.7 percent, so the deduction is 6,600 dollars. The standard rate wins there by 2,100 dollars.

Flip the inputs and the answer flips. A work truck with low insurance but heavy depreciation can land near the standard rate, while an expensive new vehicle financed at a high rate can produce an actual-expense deduction well above it. The only reliable way to know is to plug in your own figures, which is what the calculator above does.

The last step is converting the deduction into tax. A deduction is not a credit, so an 8,700 dollar write-off at a 30 percent combined marginal and self-employment rate removes roughly 2,610 dollars of tax, not 8,700. The table shows both figures separately for that reason.

Two smaller details matter more than people expect. The standard rate is meant to cover all running costs, so parking is generally not added on top of it, while tolls are usually claimed separately whichever method you use. And the moving rate applies only to active-duty military relocations; for everyone else, moving expenses have not been deductible since the 2017 tax law changed the rules.

When the Actual Expense Method Wins

The standard rate is built to approximate an average car. Vehicles far from average are where the two methods diverge, and they diverge in both directions.

  • Expensive vehicle, high business use. A 60,000 dollar truck financed at 7 percent produces interest, registration and depreciation that dwarf a flat per-mile allowance.
  • Very high annual mileage. At 30,000 miles the fixed costs get spread thin, which usually favours the standard rate. At 6,000 low-speed delivery miles in a city, fuel and wear per mile can be high enough that actual expenses win.
  • Leased vehicles. Lease payments are not part of the standard rate at all, so a leased van used mostly for work often does better on actual expenses.
  • First-year purchases. The method chosen in the first year of business use tends to lock in for the life of that vehicle.

The catch is paperwork. Actual expenses require receipts for every fill-up, repair and premium, plus a defensible business-use percentage. If your record keeping is thin, the standard rate is not just easier, it is the only method you can support.

The Mistake That Costs People Money Every Year

The most expensive habit in mileage tracking is the annual reconstruction: in March, opening a calendar and estimating that you drove about 15,000 miles for work. It is risky, because a reconstructed total is exactly what an examiner targets, and it is usually too low. People forget client visits, airport runs and supply pickups far more often than they inflate them.

The second mistake is subtler. Many drivers assume the app they use counts every deductible mile, but most apps count only miles while a trip is active or while driving for a platform. The drive to pick up a tool, the run to the bank with a business deposit, and the detour to a supplier are business miles too, and if they are never logged they simply disappear.

Treat the mileage deduction as a record-keeping project rather than a spreadsheet exercise. A daily log that takes thirty seconds, with the reason written in plain words, is worth more than any clever optimisation at filing time. It is also worth remembering that a deduction is not refundable money: it reduces income, so its value depends on your bracket and on whether you owe self-employment tax. Someone in a low-tax year gets little benefit from a large deduction, which is a good reason to spread big purchases across years rather than rush them.
