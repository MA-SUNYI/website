Ideal body weight formulas estimate a reference weight from height. The Devine formula, published in 1974 for drug dosing, remains the most widely used: 50 kg for the first 152.4 cm of height, plus 2.3 kg for each additional inch in men, with 45.5 kg and 2.3 kg as the equivalents for women.

For a 175 cm man, the first 152.4 cm accounts for 50 kg, and the remaining 22.6 cm (about 8.9 inches) adds 20.5 kg, giving roughly 70.5 kg. The same height for a woman gives about 66 kg. These are population averages derived from insurance data, not individual targets, and they say nothing about body composition.

The formulas were built for a specific population at a specific time and are known to be imprecise at the extremes of height. For anyone significantly taller or shorter than average, the linear adjustment drifts. Treated as a rough reference point alongside a healthy BMI range, they are useful; treated as a personal goal, they are not. A better range comes from combining height, build, age and measured body composition.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is ideal body weight calculated?

The Devine formula adds a base weight for the first 152.4 cm of height plus 2.3 kg per additional inch. Several other formulas exist and give slightly different figures.

Is ideal body weight the same as a healthy weight?

No. Healthy weight is a range, usually expressed as a BMI band. Ideal body weight is a single figure derived from population averages.

Do the formulas work for very tall or short people?

They become less accurate at the extremes, because the linear adjustment does not account for how body proportions change with height.

Should I use this as a weight loss target?

Better to use a healthy BMI range plus body composition measurements. A single number ignores build, age and muscle mass entirely.
