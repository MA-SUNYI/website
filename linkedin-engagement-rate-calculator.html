LinkedIn Engagement Rate Calculator

LinkedIn is the one major feed where the headline engagement number in your own analytics does not use followers as the base. The network divides reactions, comments and reposts by impressions, because the feed only shows a post to a slice of your audience and a follower-based rate would mostly measure how old your account is. This calculator uses the impressions formula, adds a follower-based figure for comparison, and splits the total so you can see how much of your engagement came from comments and reposts rather than passive reactions.

How the LinkedIn engagement rate is calculated

The formula is short, and almost every argument about it is really an argument about which denominator to use. LinkedIn's own analytics divides by impressions; agencies comparing influencers divide by followers; nobody divides by reach, because the network does not report it separately.

Rate by impressions = (reactions + comments + reposts) / impressions x 100
Rate by followers = (reactions + comments + reposts) / followers x 100
Comment share = comments / (reactions + comments + reposts) x 100

Worked example: a post with 86 reactions, 17 comments and 9 reposts on 3,200 impressions. The numerator is 112. Divided by 3,200 and multiplied by 100, the rate is 3.50 percent. Against the 2.4 percent tech and SaaS benchmark that is a solid result, though not a breakout one. If the same post sat in front of 8,000 followers, the follower-based rate would be 1.40 percent, and seen in isolation that number would look mediocre. That gap is the whole reason the impressions base matters: the post did not change, only the denominator did.

The comment share here is 17 divided by 112, or 15.2 percent. That is the number worth watching over time. Reactions cost a thumb; a comment costs a sentence with your name on it, and LinkedIn's distribution weighs comments more heavily when deciding whether to show the post to someone outside your network.

Common usage

  • Auditing a month of posts to find which format earns comments rather than applause.
  • Reporting a company page's performance in a form that matches what the platform shows internally.
  • Checking an influencer's claimed rate against a screenshot of their actual analytics.
  • Deciding whether a post should be repeated, because a high rate on low impressions means the idea was good and the timing was not.
  • Setting a realistic internal target before promising a client a number you cannot hit.

Why the denominator you choose quietly rewrites your results

Two people can look at the same LinkedIn post and honestly report 3.5 percent and 1.4 percent. Both are calculating correctly. The first divided by impressions, the second divided by followers. If the first person is presenting to a client and the second is a buyer comparing influencer rates, they will disagree about whether the post worked, and neither of them is lying.

The practical rule is to pick one denominator, state it, and never mix them in the same report. Impressions are the better choice for judging a post, because they isolate the content from the size of the account. Followers are the better choice for comparing two accounts to each other, because a buyer needs one number per creator and impressions are not public. What you should never do is calculate by followers and present the result as if it were the impressions rate, or the reverse, to make a small account look stronger than it is.

There is a second, subtler distortion: LinkedIn counts an impression every time a post appears on screen, including the same person scrolling past it several times. A post that is reshown to a narrow audience can accumulate impressions without reaching anyone new, which pushes the rate down even though nothing went wrong. This is why a falling rate across a quarter sometimes means the algorithm is re-serving your post to the same people, not that the content got worse.

Two limitations worth stating plainly. First, this tool cannot see whether impressions include your own views or your team's, which LinkedIn does count; on a small page that inflates both the numerator and the denominator unevenly. Second, engagement rate says nothing about whether the engagement was the kind you wanted. A comment section full of people disagreeing is a 6 percent rate and a marketing problem. Read a few of the comments before you celebrate the number.

Frequently asked questions

Why does LinkedIn divide engagement by impressions instead of followers?

Because the feed shows a post to a small fraction of your followers. Dividing by followers measures how big your audience is and almost nothing about the post; dividing by impressions measures how the people who actually saw it responded. LinkedIn Analytics itself uses impressions, so a follower-based rate will not match the number in your own dashboard.

Which reactions should I include in the numerator?

All six. Like, Celebrate, Support, Love, Insightful and Funny each count as one reaction and there is no weighting difference in the formula. What does change the outcome is separating comments and shares from reactions, which this calculator does, because a post with 40 reactions and 12 comments is behaving very differently from one with 50 reactions and 2.

Is a 2 percent rate good on a company page with 50,000 followers?

It can be. Company pages reliably run below personal profiles on the same content, and reach per follower falls as a page grows, so 2 percent on a large page is often healthier than 5 percent on a personal account with 800 connections. Compare your page only with other pages of a similar size and industry before drawing a conclusion.

Do reposts with a comment count differently from plain reposts?

No. Both register as one repost in the formula, even though a repost with commentary travels further because it enters the reposter's own feed as original text. If you want that difference reflected, count only commentary reposts in the numerator, but then you are measuring a different metric and cannot compare it to published benchmarks.

How many posts do I need before the average means anything?

At least ten, ideally twenty. A single post's rate swings wildly with one repost from a large account. Split the sample by format as well, because a document carousel and a text-only post are not competing for the same impressions, and averaging them together hides which format is actually doing the work.

Does engagement rate include clicks on a link I shared?

Not in the standard formula. Link clicks are reported separately as a click-through rate, and folding them in inflates the number beyond anything you can compare with a benchmark. Track clicks as their own metric; a post with a 1.8 percent engagement rate and a strong click-through is often more valuable than a post with a 5 percent rate and no clicks.
