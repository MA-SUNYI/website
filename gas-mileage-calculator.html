Fuel economy is simple arithmetic — distance divided by fuel used — but the cost of poor economy compounds in ways that are easy to underrate. A vehicle returning 24 mpg instead of 30 mpg costs about 25% more in fuel for the same distance.

For 12,000 miles a year at $3.60 per gallon: at 30 mpg the annual fuel cost is $1,440; at 24 mpg it is $1,800; at 18 mpg it is $2,400. The difference between the best and worst case is $960 a year, which over a five-year ownership is nearly $5,000 — enough to outweigh a substantial portion of the purchase price difference between models.

Measurement matters as much as the calculation. The trip-computer figure is optimistic in most vehicles, sometimes by 5%. The reliable method is to fill the tank, reset the trip meter, drive normally, then divide miles driven by the gallons needed to refill. Doing that across three tanks gives a figure accurate enough to trust.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate mpg?

Divide miles driven by gallons used to refill. Fill to full, reset the trip, drive, refill to full, then divide.

Why does my car's computer show better mileage than I calculate?

Manufacturer systems often estimate rather than measure precisely, and tend to be optimistic by a few percent. Hand calculation across several tanks is more accurate.

Does driving style really matter?

Yes, substantially. Aggressive acceleration and high-speed cruising can reduce economy by 15% to 30% compared with moderate driving.

What is the difference between mpg and L/100km?

They are inverse measures. Higher mpg is better; lower L/100km is better. Because the relationship is inverse, improving from 15 to 20 mpg saves more fuel than improving from 35 to 40 mpg.
