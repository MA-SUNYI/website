How the conversion works

Any terminating decimal is a fraction with a power of ten on the bottom. The method is the one taught in school, and it is worth knowing because it explains why some decimals turn into tidy fractions while others do not.

Suppose the decimal has n digits after the point. Multiply by 10n to shift the point all the way to the right, and write the result over 10n. Then divide top and bottom by their greatest common divisor.

Take 0.375. There are three digits, so 0.375 = 375/1000. The greatest common divisor of 375 and 1000 is 125, and dividing both by 125 gives 3/8. Nothing reduces further, so 3/8 is the answer.

Repeating decimals

Decimals such as 0.333... never terminate, so the power-of-ten trick does not apply directly. Instead you set up an equation and subtract:

  • Let x = 0.333...
  • Multiply by 10: 10x = 3.333...
  • Subtract the first line from the second: 9x = 3
  • So x = 3/9 = 1/3

The count of nines in the denominator always matches the length of the repeating block. A two digit block such as 0.272727... gives 27/99, which reduces to 3/11. This calculator detects the repeating pattern automatically once you type at least three repeated digits, so 0.333 or 0.1666 is enough.

When you actually need this

  • Woodworking and DIY — tape measures are marked in fractions while laser measures report decimals. Converting 0.875 in to 7/8 in avoids guessing between markings.
  • Scaling recipes — a kitchen scale reports 0.33 lb while a recipe asks for 1/3 lb. Exact fractions matter when baking, where small errors compound.
  • Checking homework — showing each reduction step makes it easy to spot where a hand calculation went wrong instead of only confirming the final answer.
  • Reading old drawings — engineering and architectural drawings mix decimal millimetres with fractional inches, so conversions come up constantly.
  • Mental arithmetic — recognising that 0.375 is 3/8 lets you multiply and divide quickly without reaching for a calculator.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert a decimal to a fraction by hand?

Count the digits after the decimal point, put the digits over 10, 100 or 1000 depending on that count, then divide both parts by their greatest common divisor. For 0.75: two digits means 75/100, and dividing both by 25 gives 3/4.

Can this calculator handle repeating decimals such as 0.333...

Yes. Type the repeating block at least three times, for example 0.333 or 0.1666. The tool recognises the pattern and returns the exact fraction, so 0.333 becomes 1/3 and 0.1666 becomes 1/6.

Does the calculator work with negative decimals?

Yes. A leading minus sign is preserved, so -0.5 is returned as -1/2 and -1.25 as -5/4. The reduction steps are identical, only the sign is carried through.

Why is the output always in simplest form?

Because the numerator and denominator are divided by their greatest common divisor before display. Simplest form is the standard way fractions are written in maths, on engineering drawings and in recipes, and it is the form most teachers expect.

What about decimals larger than 1, like 2.4?

They become improper fractions. 2.4 has one digit after the point, so it is 24/10, which reduces to 12/5. The step panel also shows the mixed number 2 2/5 so you can use whichever form fits the context.
