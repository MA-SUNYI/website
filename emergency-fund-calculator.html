An emergency fund is cash held for events you cannot predict: a job loss, a medical bill, a car repair, a furnace that fails in January. Its purpose is to prevent a temporary shock from becoming long-term debt, and its size is determined by how exposed the household is.

The standard guidance is three to six months of essential expenses — not income. Rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments and transport, excluding discretionary spending. A household spending $4,200 a month on essentials needs $12,600 at three months and $25,200 at six. Income stability determines where in that range you should sit: two salaries with secure employment sit at the low end; freelance, commission-based or single-income households need the upper end or beyond.

Where the money sits matters as much as how much there is. It needs to be liquid and stable — a high-yield savings account or money market fund, not equities, which can be down precisely when you need them. A common arrangement is a separate account at a different institution, which makes accidental spending less likely.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many months of expenses should I save?

Three to six months of essential expenses for stable dual-income households, six to twelve for single-income, freelance or commission-based earners.

Should the emergency fund be based on income or expenses?

Expenses. An emergency fund replaces spending, not income, and including discretionary spending inflates the target unnecessarily.

Where should I keep an emergency fund?

In something liquid and stable — a high-yield savings account or money market fund. Investments can fall exactly when you need to withdraw.

Is paying off debt more urgent than building a fund?

Usually a small buffer comes first — one month of expenses — then high-interest debt, then the full fund. Without any buffer, every unexpected cost returns to the credit card.
