How the Tire Size Calculator Works

Every tire size encodes three numbers and one letter. Take 225/45R17: 225 is the section width in millimetres, 45 is the aspect ratio — the sidewall height as a percentage of that width — R means radial, and 17 is the wheel diameter in inches. Everything else on the sidewall follows from those four values, which is why a calculator only needs them.

The chain runs in this order:

Sidewall height = Width × Aspect ÷ 100  (mm)
Overall diameter = Rim × 25.4 + 2 × Sidewall  (mm)
Circumference = Diameter × π
Revolutions per mile = 1,609,344 ÷ Circumference
Speedometer error = New diameter ÷ Old diameter − 1

Worked example. Old size 225/45R17: sidewall is 225 × 0.45 = 101.3 mm, so the diameter is 17 × 25.4 + 2 × 101.3 = 431.8 + 202.5 = 634.3 mm, or 24.97 inches. New size 225/40R18: sidewall is 90 mm, diameter is 457.2 + 180 = 637.2 mm, or 25.09 inches. The new tire is 2.9 mm taller — 0.46 percent — which is the textbook plus-one upgrade. Circumference moves from 1,992.7 mm to 2,002.0 mm, so revolutions per mile fall from 807.6 to 803.9, a difference nobody will notice.

Now the bad example. Swap that same 225/45R17 for a 205/55R16, a common "cheaper tire" move. The new diameter is 16 × 25.4 + 2 × 112.75 = 631.9 mm, which sounds close until you divide: 631.9 ÷ 634.3 is 0.996, so you are only 0.4 percent off. Run it the other way — a 215/60R16 at 664.4 mm — and the gap is 4.7 percent, well past the point where the speedometer is worth trusting. Same car, same bolt pattern, completely different result.

Reading the Numbers on the Sidewall

The section width is measured across the widest part of the inflated tire, not the tread. Two tires stamped 225 can differ by several millimetres in actual width depending on the mould and the rim they sit on, which is why the rim width range on the spec sheet matters more than the nominal number.

The aspect ratio is where most people trip. It is not a fixed height — it is a percentage of width. A 245/40 has a 98 mm sidewall, while a 205/55 has 112.75 mm. The wider, lower-profile tire is the shorter one, which is exactly backwards from how it reads at a glance. That single misunderstanding produces most of the bad plus-sizing decisions on the road.

Load index and speed rating trail the size, as in 225/45R17 94H. 94 is the load index — 1,477 lb per tire — and H is the speed rating, 130 mph. Fitting a lower load index or speed rating than the door placard specifies is not a styling choice; it is running the tire outside the specification the vehicle was certified with.

Diameter Difference Benchmarks

The percentage in the middle column is new diameter divided by old diameter, minus one. Shops and tire manufacturers generally converge on 3 percent as the outer edge of acceptable, and most of the industry stays under 2 percent when it can.

A 2 percent error sounds trivial until you run the arithmetic on a 15,000 mile year. Two percent of 15,000 is 300 miles the odometer never records — which flatters your fuel economy, shortens the apparent service interval, and quietly moves the lease return mileage in your favour or against you depending on which way the error runs.

Common Plus Sizing Steps

Plus sizing keeps the overall diameter roughly constant while trading sidewall for wheel. Each step adds an inch of rim and drops the aspect ratio to compensate. These are real fits from a 225/45R17 starting point.

What the Numbers Do Not Tell You

Offset and backspacing. A wider tire on the same wheel pushes the sidewall outward and inward by half the width increase. On a car with 8 mm of strut clearance, 10 mm more section width is a rub. The calculator compares diameters; it cannot see your suspension.

Rim width range. Every tire size has a published rim width range, and mounting outside it distorts the contact patch. A 245 on a 7 inch rim will mount and will also wear the shoulders and feel vague on turn-in.

All-wheel drive tolerance. Controllers compare wheel speeds and often allow only 2 to 3 percent difference in rolling circumference across all four corners. A staggered setup or one new pair against three worn tires can keep the centre coupling engaged continuously, which is a four-figure repair.

Speed rating and load index. Nothing in the diameter math accounts for them. A larger, heavier tire on a car specified for a 91V needs a load index that still covers the axle weight, and insurance and warranty claims have been denied over exactly this.

Spare and TPMS. A compact spare is sized to a specific diameter. Fit a taller set of tires and the spare becomes an odd wheel, which is tolerable for 50 miles at 50 mph and not much else.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much diameter difference is safe when changing tire size?

Most shops treat plus or minus 3 percent of overall diameter as the practical limit. Beyond that the speedometer reads noticeably wrong, the ABS and stability systems see wheel speeds they do not expect, and on tight cars the taller tire can rub the strut or the inner fender liner at full lock.

Does a taller tire make my speedometer read slower or faster?

A taller tire travels further per revolution, so at the same indicated speed you are actually going faster than the gauge says. Five percent larger diameter means the speedometer reads about 5 percent low — 60 mph on the dash is roughly 63 mph on the road — and the odometer under-counts by the same ratio.

Can I put a wider tire on the same rim?

Usually one step, sometimes two, inside the rim width range the tire maker publishes for that size. A 7.5 inch rim commonly accepts 215 to 235 mm section widths. Past that range the sidewall bulges, steering response dulls and the shoulders wear unevenly. Check the spec sheet instead of assuming the tyre will stretch.

What does the 225 in 225/45R17 actually measure?

225 is the section width in millimetres, taken across the widest part of the inflated tire rather than the tread face. 45 is the aspect ratio — sidewall height as a percentage of that width, so about 101 mm here. R is radial construction and 17 is the wheel diameter in inches.

Why do revolutions per mile matter on all-wheel drive cars?

The controller compares wheel speeds and expects all four corners within a small tolerance, often 2 to 3 percent of rolling circumference. Mixing a new pair with worn tires, or running different sizes front to rear, can keep the transfer case or centre differential working continuously and overheating it.

Should I run a narrower winter tire?

A slightly narrower tire of the same overall diameter puts more load per square inch on the tread, which helps it cut through slush and loose snow and reduces aquaplaning. Dropping one width size while keeping diameter inside 3 percent is a common winter setup, but it is a preference rather than a rule that suits every vehicle.
