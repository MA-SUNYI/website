Print on Demand Profit Calculator

Most print on demand losses are made before the first ad runs, at the moment a price is set against a base cost without subtracting the payment processor, the marketplace and the per-order charge. This tool takes one retail price and up to six supplier costs, prints the net profit and margin you would keep on each selling channel, and tells you the lowest price that still breaks even. The comparison matters more than any single number: the same twenty-five dollar tee can clear twelve dollars direct and six on a marketplace, and that gap decides where the next design should live.

How the Calculation Works

The tool runs the same retail price through six channel fee structures and six supplier cost rows, so you can read across a row for one product or down a column for one channel. Each cell follows one formula:

  • Revenue = retail price plus any shipping you charge the buyer. This tool assumes you do not charge separately, so revenue equals the retail price.
  • Channel fee = revenue times the channel percentage, plus the per-order fixed charge.
  • Supplier cost = base cost plus supplier shipping. Printful, Printify and similar providers bill both, and shipping is usually the larger surprise.
  • Net profit = revenue minus channel fee minus supplier cost minus ad spend per sale.
  • Margin = net profit divided by revenue.
  • Break-even price = (base cost + shipping + ad spend + fixed fee) divided by (1 minus channel percentage).

Worked example. A Bella Canvas 3001 tee with a thirteen dollar base cost and $4.69 shipping, sold at $25 on Etsy. Revenue is $25, and since the buyer is not charged shipping separately, the fee base is also $25. Etsy takes 6.5 percent as a transaction fee ($1.63), 3 percent payment processing plus a $0.25 per-order charge ($1.00), a 0.5 percent regulatory operating fee ($0.13) and a $0.20 listing renewal, for $2.95 in total, which is 9.5 percent plus forty-five cents. Supplier cost is $17.69. Net is $25 minus $2.95 minus $17.69, or $4.36, a 17.4 percent margin. Break-even is ($13 + $4.69 + $0.45) divided by 0.905, or $20.04.

The same tee on a Shopify store with Basic card rates: revenue $25, fees 2.9 percent plus $0.30 equals $1.03, supplier $17.69, net $6.29, a 25.1 percent margin. Direct sales through a card processor carry the same $1.03, because the processing rate belongs to the processor rather than the platform; what a store adds is hosting and apps, not a percentage. Amazon Handmade at fifteen percent is $3.75 in fees and $3.56 net. The spread between $3.56 and $6.29 on an identical product is the whole reason to run this comparison before printing inventory.

Why the Ads Usually Decide the Outcome

Put eight dollars of ad spend per sale into the t-shirt example and the Etsy net of $3.73 turns into a loss of $4.27. That is the most common reason a print on demand shop looks profitable in a spreadsheet and loses money at the bank. Paid traffic has to come out of the same margin the marketplace and the supplier already took a cut of, so a product that needs ads to sell has to clear a much higher price or a much lower base cost than one that sells through search.

There are two practical consequences. First, if you intend to run ads, model the ad spend per sale rather than the monthly budget: a $300 budget at forty sales is $7.50 per sale, which will wipe out a thin tee and leave a poster healthy. Second, organic channels such as search-driven marketplaces reward a lower price with more impressions, which is why the same design can be profitable on Etsy at $25 with no ads and unprofitable on a Shopify store at $25 with a $6 acquisition cost. The channel is not inherently better; the traffic source is.

What the Estimate Leaves Out

Returns are the biggest omission, and print on demand apparel returns run well above ordinary retail because buyers cannot try a size on. A five percent return rate on a fourteen percent margin erases more than a third of the profit, since you lose the shipping and often the print as well. Second, embroidery, oversized sizes and dark garments with a large print area all add to base cost, sometimes by two to four dollars, and none of that is in the row you typed. Third, sales tax collection and remittance, subscription fees for a platform, and design software are business-level costs; they belong in a monthly total, not in a per-item table.

The honest conclusion from running this for a while is that print on demand is a design business with narrow margins, not a manufacturing business with wide ones. Base costs belong to the supplier, fees belong to the platform, and the only parts you control are the price, the niche and the traffic source. If those three do not line up, a better spreadsheet will not fix it.

Frequently Asked Questions

My shirt sells for 25 and the base cost is 13. Why is my profit only about 6?

Payment processing takes about three percent, the marketplace takes six and a half, and there are listing and per-order charges on top. On a $25 sale with a $13 base cost and $4.69 shipping, about $6 is what remains, and that is before ads.

Should I price for margin or for the lowest price my niche will bear?

Price for margin first, then change the design or the supplier. Base costs are fixed by the provider, so price and channel are the only levers. If the niche cannot support twenty-five percent after fees, the product is a poor fit.

Why do hoodies look more profitable than t-shirts here?

Percentage fees scale with price while supplier cost rises more slowly, so a $45 hoodie with a $22 base cost can clear more than a $25 tee with a $9 base cost. The trade-off is that fewer people buy a hoodie on impulse.

Does Amazon Handmade look better or worse than the other channels?

Worse on percentage, better on volume. The fifteen percent referral fee is more than double Etsy's transaction rate, but the traffic already exists. Whether the volume pays for the fee depends on your niche.

What break-even price should I use as a hard floor?

Take the break-even figure and add a buffer for promo codes and small refund rates. A floor that exactly matches break-even loses money every time a coupon is applied.

Is supplier shipping per item or per order?

Enter it per item, which is how providers quote it. Multi-item orders usually ship for less than the per-item figure times quantity, so a two-shirt order beats this single-item table.
