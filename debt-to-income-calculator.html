Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is the share of gross monthly income committed to debt payments. It is the single most important number in a mortgage application, and it is calculated two ways that lenders use for different purposes.

Front-end DTI covers housing only — mortgage principal, interest, taxes, insurance and HOA dues. Back-end DTI includes housing plus every other obligation: car loans, student loans, credit card minimums, personal loans. On $7,500 gross monthly income with a $1,800 mortgage and $700 in other debt, the front-end ratio is 24% and the back-end is 33%. Both sit comfortably within conventional limits.

The thresholds are strict in practice: 36% or below is strong, 43% is the usual maximum for a qualified mortgage, and above 43% most conventional lenders decline. Student loan reporting causes frequent surprises, because lenders often count 1% of the outstanding balance as a monthly payment even when the actual payment is income-driven and currently zero.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good debt-to-income ratio?

Below 36% is generally considered strong. 43% is the usual maximum for a qualified mortgage, and above that most conventional lenders will decline the application.

Does DTI include rent or only mortgage debt?

It includes whatever housing payment applies. For a buyer it is the proposed mortgage; for a renter applying for credit, rent is usually included in the calculation.

How do student loans affect DTI?

Lenders often count 1% of the outstanding balance as a monthly obligation, even if the actual payment is lower or currently paused under an income-driven plan.

Can I lower my DTI quickly?

Paying down a loan balance or consolidating cards reduces the reported monthly obligation. Increasing documented income has the same effect and sometimes works faster.
