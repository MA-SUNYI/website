Board Foot Calculator

Hardwood is not priced by the piece or by the square foot. It is priced by the board foot, a unit that mixes thickness, width and length into one number so a narrow 8/4 plank and a wide 4/4 plank can sit on the same invoice. This calculator converts your dimensions and quantity into board feet, tells you what the load costs at your price per board foot, and estimates the weight so you know whether the trip home needs a trailer.

How the Board Foot Math Works

The definition is fixed: one board foot is 144 cubic inches, the volume of a board 1 inch thick, 12 inches wide and 12 inches long. The formula just counts how many 144 cubic inch blocks your board contains.

Board feet = (thickness in inches x width in inches x length in feet) / 12, measured on each piece, then multiplied by the number of pieces. Length stays in feet on purpose: since a board foot is 12 inches long, dividing by 12 folds the inch-to-foot conversion into the same step.

Worked example. You need ten boards at 8/4 (2 inches) thick, 6 inches wide and 8 feet long. Each board is 2 x 6 x 8 / 12 = 8 board feet. Ten boards is 80 board feet. At $9.50 per board foot of black walnut that is $760.00, and at 38 pounds per cubic foot the load weighs about 253 pounds, which is 80 / 12 = 6.67 cubic feet times 38.

A second check with dimensional lumber. A nominal 2x6 that is 12 feet long is 2 x 6 x 12 / 12 = 12 board feet. Three of them is 36 board feet. Since a 2x6 actually measures 1-1/2 by 5-1/2 inches, its real volume is 1.5 x 5.5 x 12 / 12 = 8.25 board feet, so about 31 percent of what you paid for is air, planing and drying. That gap is why framing lumber is quoted by the piece and hardwood by the board foot.

Weight per board foot follows the same cubic foot logic. Softwoods such as white pine (about 23 lb per cubic foot) run near 1.9 pounds per board foot, while hard maple at 44 lb per cubic foot runs near 3.7 pounds per board foot, a two-times spread on the same invoice.

Common Usage

  • Pricing a furniture build before you drive to the yard, so the quote at the counter matches what you budgeted.
  • Comparing two suppliers who quote different widths, since board feet normalise 4 inch and 10 inch stock to one unit.
  • Deciding between 4/4 and 8/4 for a tabletop: a 2 inch slab is double the board feet of a 1 inch board of the same face area.
  • Sizing a vehicle. Anything above roughly 300 board feet of green hardwood usually needs a trailer or a delivery fee.
  • Estimating drying loss. Kiln-dried stock loses about 6 to 8 percent of its width, which shows up as fewer usable board feet on the finished piece.
  • Planning a deck frame: use the deck board calculator for surfacing, and this page for the joists and beams.

Why Board Feet Still Confuse Buyers

The most common mistake is using the wrong thickness. A 4/4 board is nominally 1 inch, but many yards bill it at 1-1/8 inches to cover rough milling, which quietly adds 12.5 percent. A 40 board foot order becomes 45. Ask what billing thickness the yard uses before you compare prices, and put that number into the thickness field.

The second mistake is treating nominal and actual size as the same thing on construction lumber. A 2x10 nominal is 1.5 by 9.25 inches, not 2 by 10, so its real volume is about 13 percent smaller than the nominal board foot figure. Both conventions are legitimate; mixing them is not.

The third is forgetting waste. Board feet describe the lumber you buy, not the parts you get. A furniture project with 20 percent cut-off waste and a 10 percent planing loss needs about 1.4 times the finished board feet. The calculator gives you the honest starting number, but you should add your own waste factor before you order.

Finally, board feet say nothing about grade or figure. A Select and Better board and a Number 2 Common board of the same size are the same board feet but not the same value, so compare price per board foot only within the same grade and species.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Do I measure board feet with nominal or actual lumber sizes? Milled hardwood is sold by its actual measured size, so a 4/4 board that planes down to about 13/16 inch still bills as 1 inch thick. Construction lumber is the opposite: a 2x4 is a 2x4 on the price tag but measures 1-1/2 by 3-1/2 inches. Bill dimensional lumber on the nominal figures, and bill rough hardwood on the size you actually cut.
  • Why does my hardwood quote not match the calculator when I enter 1 inch thickness? Many yards add a rough-milling allowance on top of the nominal quarter. A 4/4 board may be billed at 1-1/8 inch, a 6/4 at 1-5/8 inch and an 8/4 at 2-1/8 inch, which raises board feet by 12 to 12.5 percent. Use the yard's billing thickness, not the quarter number.
  • How much does 100 board feet of walnut weigh? Black walnut averages about 38 pounds per cubic foot at 12 percent moisture content, and a board foot is one twelfth of a cubic foot, so 100 board feet weighs roughly 317 pounds. Add the driver and tools and a half-ton pickup is close to its limit.
  • Does a wider board cost more per board foot? Usually yes, but not because of the board foot math. Wide boards come from wider logs, yield less clear material and are graded more harshly, so 8 inch and up lines often carry a higher price per board foot than 4 inch stock of the same species and grade.
  • How many board feet are in a log? The Doyle rule still on many scale sticks reads roughly 94 board feet for a 16 foot log with a 16 inch small-end diameter, while the International 1/4 inch rule reads about 180. Mill yield is lower than scale because of slabs, edging and defects, so log scale is an upper bound.
  • Can I use board feet for plywood and sheet goods? No. Plywood, MDF and particle board are sold by the sheet or by the square foot, with thickness fixed by the manufacturer. Applying board feet to a 3/4 inch 4 by 8 foot sheet would give 8 board feet, which nobody quotes, so keep sheet goods out of this math.
