BMI weight categories map a body mass index onto the four standard bands used by health agencies: under 18.5 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 is normal, 25.0 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30.0 and above is obese. The cut-offs are identical across the WHO adult classifications, which makes the band easy to apply once the index is known.

The bands exist because BMI correlates with health outcomes across large populations. They were never designed to describe an individual precisely, and the same index value can mean very different things at different body compositions. A 1.83 m athlete weighing 95 kg has a BMI of 28.4 — squarely in the overweight band — with a body-fat percentage in single digits. The category is technically correct and practically misleading.

Two systematic biases are worth knowing. The thresholds were derived from predominantly Western populations, and several health bodies now apply adjusted cut-offs for people of South and East Asian descent, where cardiometabolic risk rises at lower index values. And BMI cannot distinguish fat mass from lean mass at all, which is why waist circumference or body-fat measurement adds real information rather than duplicating the same number.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the BMI categories for adults?

Under 18.5 underweight, 18.5–24.9 normal weight, 25.0–29.9 overweight, 30.0 and above obese. These are the WHO adult bands.

Are BMI categories accurate for athletes?

No. Muscle is denser than fat, so muscular people frequently land in the overweight band with low body fat. Waist measurement or a body-fat estimate gives a better picture.

Are the categories the same for everyone?

Not exactly. Some health bodies apply lower thresholds for people of South and East Asian descent, where metabolic risk appears at lower BMI values.

Does a normal BMI mean I am healthy?

It is one signal among several. Blood pressure, blood glucose, waist circumference, fitness and diet all carry information BMI cannot capture.
