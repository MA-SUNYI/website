Voltage Drop Calculator

A 100 foot run of 14 AWG carrying a 12 amp saw loses about 7.5 volts before the current ever reaches the tool. That is 6.3 percent of a 120 volt circuit, enough to make a motor run hot and a light dim, and it is the reason electricians size long runs by distance rather than by ampacity alone. Enter the conductor, the one-way length, the load current and the system voltage, and this tool reports the volts lost, the percentage drop, the voltage the equipment actually sees, and how many feet of that conductor would stay inside 3 percent.

The Formula Behind the Numbers

Voltage drop is Ohm's law applied to the conductor instead of the load. The conductor has resistance, current flows through it, and the product of the two is the voltage that never reaches the equipment.

Single phase: Vdrop = 2 x K x I x L / CM, where K is the resistivity in circular mil ohms per foot, I is current in amps, L is one-way length in feet and CM is the circular mil area of the conductor. Three phase: Vdrop = 1.732 x K x I x L / CM. The 2 and the 1.732 are the reason single phase counts the return path and three phase splits the load across three conductors.

This page uses the resistance-per-1000-feet method, which is the same arithmetic written differently: Vdrop = I x R x 2L / 1000 for single phase, with R taken from the copper or aluminum resistance table at 75 degrees Celsius. For 12 AWG copper R is 1.93 ohms per 1000 feet, so 20 amps over 150 feet of conductor (75 feet each way) gives 20 x 1.93 x 0.150 = 5.79 volts, which is 4.83 percent of 120 volts. The tool reports 114.2 volts at the load and tells you that 46 feet is the longest one-way run that holds 3 percent at 20 amps.

Worked example two, a 240 volt well pump at 8 amps on 10 AWG copper, 250 feet away. R for 10 AWG is 1.21 ohms per 1000 feet. Vdrop = 8 x 1.21 x 0.5 = 4.84 volts, or 2.02 percent, leaving 235.2 volts at the pump. That passes. Drop the wire to 14 AWG (3.14 ohms per 1000 feet) and the same run loses 12.56 volts, 5.23 percent, which is why a pump that trips on low voltage is usually a wire size problem rather than a pump problem.

Common Usage

  • Sizing a subpanel feeder from the house to a detached garage or shop, where 100 to 150 foot runs are normal.
  • Checking a well pump, irrigation controller or gate opener at the far end of a rural property.
  • Planning level 2 EV charging, where a 40 amp continuous load over a long driveway run is a classic drop problem.
  • Deciding between 120 and 240 volts for a woodworking shop or welder in an outbuilding.
  • Verifying a lighting circuit where the last fixture on a 150 foot run flickers when the heater cycles.

Why This Matters More Than the Manufacturer Admits

Most equipment is rated to operate within 10 percent of nominal voltage, and motors inside the voltage tolerance band described in NEMA MG 1, but running near the bottom of that band is expensive. An induction motor operating at 90 percent of nameplate voltage draws more current for the same mechanical output, runs hotter, and loses roughly 10 percent of its lifespan for every 10 degrees Celsius of extra winding temperature. A compressor that starts hard on a long run is often a voltage drop symptom, not a compressor defect.

Electronic loads are less forgiving in a different way. Switch mode supplies, LED drivers and variable frequency drives may accept 100 to 240 volts, but the current they draw rises as input voltage falls, which increases drop further and can push a marginal circuit into shutdown on the hottest day of the year. That is why the drop should be calculated at the full load current, not the nameplate rating of the breaker.

The most common error in the field is using one-way length in the formula and forgetting the return conductor, which halves the answer and makes a failing run look acceptable. The second is sizing the conductor for the breaker instead of the load: a 20 amp breaker on 12 AWG feeding a 16 amp continuous load has a different drop profile than the same wire feeding a 2 amp LED fixture, and only the load current belongs in the calculation. The third is ignoring ambient temperature, since a conductor at 75 degrees Celsius has about 20 percent more resistance than the same conductor at 20 degrees.

A last limitation worth stating plainly: this calculator assumes a single conductor per phase and no parallel runs. Parallel conductors divide the drop by the number of sets, and transformers, reactors and long service drops have their own impedance that this tool does not model. For a service entrance or anything above 600 volts, use the engineered drawings.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Why does the calculator double the one-way length? Current travels out on the hot conductor and back on the neutral, so the loop resistance is that of both conductors. A 100 foot run of 12 AWG has 200 feet of copper in the circuit. Enter the one-way distance and let the tool double it, or the drop comes out at half the real value.
  • Does a voltage drop over 3 percent violate the electrical code? Usually not. The 3 percent branch circuit and 5 percent total figures are informational notes in Article 210 and Article 215 of the NEC, which are guidance rather than enforceable rules; ampacity limits in Article 310 and 240 are enforceable. Some local amendments and equipment warranties do make drop mandatory, so confirm with the authority having jurisdiction.
  • Why does 240 volt wiring drop less than 120 volt wiring with the same load? The drop in volts depends on current. A 1,800 watt load draws 15 amps at 120 volts but 7.5 amps at 240 volts, so the loss falls to about a quarter and the percentage drop falls by the same factor. That is why long runs to wells and shops are wired at 240 volts.
  • Why does the same aluminum wire drop more than copper? Aluminum has about 64 percent more resistance for the same cross section, roughly 21.2 against 12.9 circular mil ohms per foot at 75 degrees Celsius. The usual fix is to go up two AWG sizes, such as 8 AWG aluminum in place of 10 AWG copper, which also removes much of the drop.
  • Is the voltage drop worse in summer heat? Yes. Copper resistance rises about 0.4 percent per degree Celsius, so a conductor at 75 degrees Celsius carries roughly 21 percent more resistance than at 20 degrees and drops 21 percent more voltage. Attic runs and crowded conduits are where this shows up.
  • Can I fix a failed voltage drop check by increasing the breaker size? No. A larger breaker lets more current flow before tripping and does nothing about resistance along the wire. The real fixes are a larger conductor, a shorter run, a higher system voltage, or moving the load closer to the source.
