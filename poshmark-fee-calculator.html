Poshmark Fee Calculator

Poshmark has the shortest fee schedule of any major resale platform and one of the highest rates: a flat $2.95 under $15, then 20% of the sale price with no cap, and payment processing folded into that single number. There is no listing fee, no monthly plan and no separate processing line to add up, which makes the platform easy to model and easy to misprice. This calculator shows the commission, what lands in your payout, your profit after item cost and any shipping you cover, and the list price needed to keep a target amount.

Result:

US two-tier schedule checked November 2026: $2.95 under $15, 20% at $15 and above. Buyers pay a $7.97 flat label up to 5 lb, so leave the shipping field at 0 unless you offered a discount or free shipping.

How the fee is calculated

The rule is two lines long. Poshmark looks at the final order price, after any accepted offer or price drop, and applies one of two formulas:

Price under $15: fee = $2.95
Price of $15 or more: fee = price x 20%

A $50 sale is $10 in commission and $40 in your payout. A $10 sale is $2.95 in commission, which is 29.5% of the money, and $7.05 in payout. The commission base is the item price, not the shipping the buyer pays, because the label is billed to the buyer separately. Bundles and multi-item orders are judged on the combined order total, so three $7 pieces sold together are a $21 order at 20%, not three orders at $2.95 each.

Going backwards is simpler than on most platforms because there is only one rate above the threshold. Divide the payout you want by 0.8 to get the list price, and above the threshold that answer is exact. To keep $40 you list at $50. To keep $12 you could list at $14.95, which pays $2.95 and leaves $12.00 exactly, or at $15.00, which pays $3.00 and leaves the same $12.00.

The $15 cliff

The two tiers meet badly. The 20% rate only exceeds the flat $2.95 fee above $14.75, so there is a narrow band where raising your price costs you money.

The practical reading is not to price at $14.99 for the arbitrage, which is trivial, but to avoid the whole $10 to $14.99 range: the flat fee takes 20% to 30% of those sales, and the same goods bundled into one order clear the threshold and drop the take to a clean 20%.

Shipping is the second fee nobody models

The buyer pays $7.97 for a prepaid USPS label covering packages up to 5 lb, and that is not part of your commission. When you send an offer to likers you can attach a discount, and Poshmark takes the difference out of your payout rather than absorbing it. A $4.99 shipping offer costs you $2.98. Free shipping costs you the full $7.97.

On a $20 item, free shipping turns a $16.00 payout into $8.03, so the platform and the label together take 59.9% of the sale. On a $100 item the same free shipping leaves $72.03, or 28.0% gone. That is why free shipping belongs on bundles and higher-priced pieces, not on a $15 single, where the label is worth more than the commission. Packages over 5 lb need a label upgrade, which is deducted from your payout and can quietly erase the margin on a heavy coat.

Pricing around the fee

Because the rate is flat above $15, every pricing decision reduces to one question: can this order clear the threshold as a single shipment? Three $7 items sold separately pay $8.85 in fees. The same three as a $21 bundle pay $4.20, saving $4.65 on the same inventory. That is the entire case for bundling small pieces, and it only works when the combined order still fits the standard label.

Above $15 the fee never gets better. There is no volume discount, no cap and no reduction for high-value items, so a $300 sale costs $60 whether it is your first or your thousandth. On anything above roughly $150, it is worth pricing the same item on a platform charging a lower percentage plus a fixed fee, then comparing the net rather than the headline rate. Poshmark's audience and flat-rate shipping often justify the twenty points on fashion, less often on electronics or furniture.

The mistake that costs the most

New sellers price from the market and discover the fee afterwards. A dress that sells for $45 on Poshmark returns $36, not $45, and if it cost $18 at the thrift store the real profit is $18, which is a 40% margin rather than the 60% the price tag implied. Multiply that error across a closet of 200 items and the shortfall is thousands of dollars of expected profit that never arrives.

The correction is to work from the payout, not the price. Decide the minimum you will accept after fees, divide by 0.8, and treat that number as your floor. Offers below the floor are declined rather than negotiated, and free shipping is priced as a $7.97 discount before you decide whether the sale is still worth taking. It is a colder way to sell, and it is the only way a 20% commission stays a business cost instead of a surprise.
