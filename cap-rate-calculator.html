Cap Rate Calculator

A cap rate is the one number that lets you compare a duplex in Ohio with a strip mall in Texas without arguing about financing. This calculator builds net operating income from rent, a vacancy allowance and real operating costs, divides it by price, then reverses the formula to show what the same income is worth at the cap rate you require. It also flags the case most buyers miss: when the loan costs more than the cap rate, borrowing makes the return worse, not better.

How the Cap Rate Calculator Works

Cap rate is built in two steps, and almost every bad valuation skips the first one.

Step 1, net operating income. Start with gross annual rent, subtract a vacancy and credit loss allowance, add any other recurring income such as parking, laundry or storage, then subtract operating expenses. Operating expenses are the costs of running the building: property tax, insurance, repairs, maintenance, property management, owner-paid utilities, HOA dues and a reserve for replacing the roof or the furnace. Mortgage payments, depreciation, income tax and one-off capital improvements are not operating expenses.

NOI = (gross annual rent − vacancy loss) + other income − operating expenses

Step 2, divide by price.

Cap rate = NOI ÷ price × 100

Because the formula is linear, it can be run backwards. If you know the income and the cap rate buyers in that market accept, you get a value: value = NOI ÷ target cap rate. That is how an appraiser reconciles an income approach and how you decide whether a listing is priced above or below its income.

Worked example. A $350,000 single-family rental rents for $2,800 a month, or $33,600 a year. Allow 5 percent for vacancy, which is $1,680, leaving effective gross income of $31,920. Taxes, insurance, management, maintenance and a repair reserve total $9,500, so NOI is $22,420. Divide by $350,000 and the cap rate is 6.41 percent. At a target cap rate of 6.5 percent, that same $22,420 of income supports a value of $344,923, so the asking price is roughly $5,000 above what the income justifies. The gross rent multiplier, price divided by gross annual rent, is 10.4.

The expense ratio in that example is 29.8 percent of effective gross income, which is on the lean side for a single-family home that pays a manager. Push expenses to $12,000 and the cap rate falls to 5.69 percent on the same price, a 0.72 point swing from one line item. That sensitivity is why the expense list deserves more attention than the rent estimate.

What Counts as a Normal Cap Rate

Cap rates are local and asset-specific, so treat these ranges as a starting point for a conversation with a broker, not a rule. Lower numbers mean buyers are paying more per dollar of income, usually because they expect rent growth or a safer location.

Class A apartment, coastal metro4.0% – 5.5%Rent growth, institutional demand
Class B/C apartment, Midwest or Sun Belt5.5% – 7.0%Higher expenses, thinner buyer pool
Single-family rental5.0% – 7.5%Small deal size, retail buyers
Retail, net lease5.5% – 7.5%Tenant credit and lease term
Self-storage5.5% – 7.5%Low operating cost, high turnover
Small office or mixed-use7.0% – 10.0%Vacancy risk, single-tenant exposure

A useful sanity check is the spread over the 10-year Treasury yield. Look up the current yield, then ask whether the extra return covers the risk of owning a building you cannot sell in a week. When the spread compresses toward one percentage point, buyers are paying for appreciation rather than income.

Why Cap Rate Is Not Your Return

It ignores debt. Two buyers can pay the same price for the same building and earn very different returns, because one used 25 percent down and the other paid cash. Cap rate deliberately hides that difference so the property can be compared on its own merits. If you want the return on your money, use cash-on-cash, which divides annual pre-tax cash flow by the cash you actually put in.

It cuts both ways with leverage. When the loan rate is below the cap rate, borrowing amplifies your return. When the loan rate is above the cap rate, borrowing subtracts from it, the condition known as negative leverage. A 6.4 percent cap rate financed at 7.5 percent on 75 percent of the price produces a cash-on-cash return well below 6.4 percent, and each extra point of rate makes it worse. In a market where cap rates have compressed while loan rates have not, this is not a theoretical problem.

It prices today only. A 4.5 percent cap rate in a supply-constrained neighborhood may beat a 9 percent cap rate next to a plant that is closing. Cap rate tells you what current income costs; it says nothing about rent growth, capital needs or exit value. Treat the number as a screening filter and read the rent roll before you trust it.

The expense list is where deals are won. Sellers present NOI with a repair budget that has never been spent. Add a reserve of 8 to 10 percent of gross rent for capital replacements, use the actual property tax reassessment rather than the seller's legacy bill, and price insurance at what a new owner would pay. Those three adjustments routinely move a cap rate by a full point.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should the mortgage payment be included in cap rate?

No. Cap rate measures the property before financing, so loan principal and interest are excluded. Putting a mortgage payment into operating expenses makes the cap rate depend on how the buyer financed, which destroys comparability between two otherwise identical buildings.

What vacancy rate should I use for a single-family rental?

Most US markets support 5 to 8 percent for a well-located home, and the allowance should cover both empty months and credit loss. Tighter markets justify the low end, but a zero vacancy assumption is almost never honest over a ten-year hold.

Is a higher cap rate always the better deal?

No. A 9 percent cap rate in a declining neighborhood can underperform a 5 percent cap rate in a supply-constrained one. Cap rate prices current income and says nothing about rent growth or resale value, so read it as a risk signal rather than a score.

How do I turn a cap rate into a purchase offer?

Divide net operating income by the cap rate you require, then subtract closing costs and immediate repairs. A building producing $24,000 of NOI supports about $342,857 at a 7 percent requirement, before those adjustments.

Does cap rate work for a duplex or a fourplex?

Yes, and it works best there. Small multifamily is valued mainly on income, so one cap rate built from all units' rent and a shared expense list compares fairly with similar buildings nearby. Charge market rent for any unit the owner occupies.

What is a good operating expense ratio?

For US residential rentals, 35 to 45 percent of effective gross income is typical once taxes, insurance, management, maintenance and a repair reserve are counted. A ratio far below 30 percent usually means something is missing from the expense list.
