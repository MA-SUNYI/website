Pregnancy Due Date Calculator

A due date is an estimate built from a dating rule, not a measurement of the baby. This calculator applies the three rules clinicians actually use: 280 days from the first day of your last period, 266 days from a known conception date, and 261 or 263 days from an IVF transfer. It also converts the result into the number you will hear at every appointment, gestational age in weeks and days, and marks the trimester boundaries so you know which window you are in today.

Every date below is calculated in whole days from the starting point you enter, so a 32 day cycle or a day 3 embryo transfer is handled the same way a textbook 28 day cycle is. No account, no stored data, nothing leaves the page.

How the Due Date Is Calculated

The standard rule, often called Naegele's rule, is 280 days from the first day of the last menstrual period. It works backwards from an assumption: a 28 day cycle with ovulation and fertilisation on day 14, then 266 days of development. That is why the two numbers differ by 14 days.

When the cycle is not 28 days, the assumption breaks and the date has to be corrected. The adjustment is simple: add the difference between your cycle length and 28 to the period-based date. A 35 day cycle adds 7 days, a 26 day cycle subtracts 2 days, and a 32 day cycle adds 4. The calculator does this automatically and then recalculates gestational age from the adjusted date, so the week count stays consistent with the due date instead of drifting apart.

A worked example: last period started 1 March 2026 on a 28 day cycle. 280 days later is 6 December 2026, so the due date is 6 December. The estimated conception date is 266 days before that, 15 March, which is exactly day 14 of the cycle. If the same person reported a 32 day cycle, ovulation would be expected around 18 March, the due date would move to 10 December, and the conception estimate moves with it. Nothing about the pregnancy changed, only the dating input.

IVF is the one case where the start point is known to the day. A day 5 blastocyst has already spent five days developing in the lab, so the transfer date plus 261 days equals the due date. A day 3 embryo gets 263 days instead. Both come from the same 266 day post-conception count, minus the days already elapsed before transfer. Gestational age is then counted back to an imaginary last period 14 days before conception, which is why an IVF pregnancy is described in the same weeks as any other.

Common Usage

  • Booking prenatal visits: the first appointment is usually placed around 8 weeks, so the calculator tells you the date range to ask for.
  • Planning leave and childcare: work out the earliest realistic start of maternity leave at 37 weeks rather than the headline due date.
  • Cross-checking a dating scan: if the clinic's estimate is a week away from yours, the difference usually points to a late ovulation rather than an error on either side.
  • Comparing IVF protocols: switching between a day 3 and a day 5 transfer shifts the due date by two days, which matters when you are coordinating a frozen cycle.
  • Working out gestational age at a past event, such as a medication taken before the pregnancy was confirmed.

Why One Date Is a Range, Not a Deadline

The most common mistake is treating the due date as a scheduled delivery. It is the centre of a distribution. Spontaneous labour clusters between 37 and 42 weeks, and only about 4 percent of babies are born on the calculated date. A first pregnancy tends to run a few days past the estimate on average; later pregnancies tend to run slightly earlier. Neither is a problem.

The second mistake is assuming the earliest date is the most accurate. It is not necessarily. A period date depends on a regular cycle and a remembered start day, and both are often approximate. A scan between 8 and 13 weeks measures the embryo directly and is usually more reliable, which is why clinicians often redate when the two disagree by more than about five to seven days. After 14 weeks the measurement becomes less precise, so a late scan rarely overrides an early one.

The third mistake is applying a cycle correction to a conception date or a transfer date. If conception is known, the cycle length is irrelevant and adding days for a long cycle double-counts. This calculator only applies the cycle adjustment to the last-period method for that reason.

One honest limitation: this tool dates a pregnancy, it does not assess it. It cannot tell you whether a pregnancy is progressing normally, and a date that conflicts with a scan is a conversation for your clinician, not something to resolve by re-entering different numbers until one looks better.

Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate is a due date based on my last period?

It is accurate to within about a week for someone with regular 28 day cycles, and less so otherwise. The whole rule rests on ovulation happening on day 14. If you ovulated on day 20, the period-based date is about six days early, and the calculator's cycle-length field is the way to correct for that without waiting for a scan.

My cycle is 35 days, why does that move my due date?

Because a longer cycle pushes ovulation later. The tool adds 7 days for a 35 day cycle, so both the due date and every milestone move by a week. Gestational age is then counted from the adjusted start, which is why the week number stays believable against the ultrasound rather than running ahead of it.

How is an IVF due date calculated?

From the transfer date plus the days the embryo still needed. A day 5 blastocyst gets 261 days, a day 3 embryo gets 263. Both assume the same 266 days from conception, so the two methods stay consistent with each other and with naturally dated pregnancies.

What is the difference between gestational age and fetal age?

Gestational age counts from the first day of the last period and is about two weeks longer than fetal age, which counts from conception. A pregnancy described as 10 weeks gestational is roughly 8 weeks fetal. Clinicians use gestational age almost exclusively, so that is the number this calculator reports.

When does each trimester start and end?

First trimester: week 0 to the end of week 13. Second: week 14 to the end of week 27. Third: week 28 until delivery. Month-based definitions vary by source and often overlap, which is why week-based boundaries are safer when you are scheduling anything.

Can my due date change after an early ultrasound?

Yes, and it often does. Before 14 weeks the scan measures the embryo and is usually the better estimate, so many practices redate when it differs from the period date by more than five to seven days. Once a scan date is adopted, later scans generally do not change it, because third-trimester measurements carry a much wider margin.
