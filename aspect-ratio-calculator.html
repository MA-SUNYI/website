Aspect Ratio Calculator

Two numbers describe every image and video frame: its shape and its size. Aspect ratio is the shape, and it survives any resize as long as both dimensions move by the same factor. This calculator reduces a width and height to the smallest whole-number ratio, tells you how far that shape sits from the standard broadcast and social formats, and returns the exact matching dimension once you lock a ratio and type one side. It also shows the megapixel count, which is what actually predicts how heavy the file will be.

How the Aspect Ratio Calculator Works

The ratio itself is one division. Frame aspect ratio equals width divided by height, so 1920 × 1080 gives 1.7777. That decimal is exact enough for engineering, but humans read shapes better as pairs, which is why the same value is written 16:9. To get there, divide both dimensions by their greatest common divisor: the GCD of 1920 and 1080 is 120, so 1920 ÷ 120 = 16 and 1080 ÷ 120 = 9.

Ratio = width ÷ height
Simplified pair = (width ÷ GCD) : (height ÷ GCD)
Missing side = known side × (other side of the ratio ÷ its partner)
Megapixels = (width × height) ÷ 1,000,000

Worked example. You have a 3840 × 2160 export and need a 1280-pixel-wide version that keeps the shape. The ratio reduces to 16:9, so the height is 1280 × 9 ÷ 16 = 720. The original frame is 3840 × 2160 ÷ 1,000,000 = 8.29 MP, and the smaller copy is 0.92 MP, about 11 percent of the pixels. Same shape, one ninth of the data.

Now a case where the shape is not the standard one. A photo straight out of a full-frame camera is 6000 × 4000, which reduces by a GCD of 2000 to 3:2. Crop it to a square for a marketplace listing and you keep 4000 × 4000, discarding a third of the width. That loss is invisible in the calculator but very visible in a product shot where the item sat near the edge.

Why the Ratio Matters More Than the Resolution

Resolution decides how sharp an image looks. Ratio decides whether it looks correct at all. A 4K file at the wrong shape will be letterboxed, pillarboxed or stretched by the platform, and stretching is the only one of the three that actively damages the picture: faces widen, circles become ovals, and no amount of sharpening fixes it afterwards.

The most common mistake is treating the pixel count as the goal. Someone exports at 1080 × 1080 because that is the number they were told, then pads a 16:9 clip into it and ships a video whose actual picture occupies 56 percent of the frame height. The file is technically the right size and practically unusable, because on a phone the subject is now smaller than the interface around it.

The second mistake is mixing up ratio and resolution when comparing specs. A 1280 × 720 video and a 3840 × 2160 video are the same shape at different densities; a 1440 × 1080 video is a different shape entirely despite having more pixels than the first. If you only remember one rule, remember that ratio is division and resolution is multiplication.

One limitation worth stating plainly: platforms re-encode and sometimes re-crop what you upload, so the ratio you deliver is a request, not a guarantee. Vertical feeds in particular may trim the top and bottom of a 9:16 file that carries text near the edges. Keep critical content inside a safe margin and treat the calculator as the start of the check, not the end of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 1920 × 1080 exactly 16:9?

Yes. Both numbers divide by 120 to give 16 and 9, and 16 ÷ 9 matches 1920 ÷ 1080 to every decimal place. Not every "HD" size is exact, though: 1366 × 768 reduces to 683:384, which is 1.779, about 0.05 percent wider than true 16:9, so a pixel-perfect fit leaves a sliver of a bar.

How much do I lose cropping 16:9 to 9:16?

Starting from 1920 × 1080, a full-height vertical crop is 607.5 pixels wide. You keep 31.6 percent of the width and discard 68.4 percent, which is why the operation is usually a reframe rather than a crop.

Why is 21:9 not the same as 2.39:1?

21 ÷ 9 is 2.333 and scope is 2.390, a gap of about 2.4 percent. Real ultrawide panels are often 2560 × 1080, which is 2.370. Small differences, but they decide whether a 2.39:1 master fills the screen or shows hairline bars.

Does resizing change the aspect ratio?

Not if both dimensions are multiplied by the same number. It changes the moment you type one dimension and leave the other, which is exactly how stretched images get made. Lock the ratio first, then pick the size you need.

Should I crop or add bars to fit another ratio?

Crop when the edges hold nothing you need: it fills the frame and reads as native on the platform. Letterbox when the edges matter, accepting that bars shrink the subject, which on a vertical feed can make the picture look like a thumbnail inside a black rectangle.

What is pixel aspect ratio, and does it affect this calculation?

Pixel aspect ratio describes the shape of a single pixel, not the frame. Standard HD uses square pixels, so this tool's maths applies directly. Legacy anamorphic footage does not: a 720 × 480 file with a 1.21 pixel aspect ratio displays as 16:9 even though the stored numbers reduce to 3:2.
