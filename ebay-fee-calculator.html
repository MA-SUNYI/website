eBay Fee Calculator

eBay looks simple: one percentage and one small fixed charge. The trouble is the base that percentage is applied to. eBay charges its final value fee on the item price, the shipping the buyer pays and the sales tax it collects on your behalf, then adds a per-order fee that lands hardest on cheap items. This calculator runs the whole stack on one sale and shows what actually reaches your bank account.

Enter your price, shipping and cost. Pick your category and store status. Add a promoted listings rate if you use it, and the tool returns the fee breakdown, your payout, your profit per sale, the effective fee rate and the lowest price at which the sale stops losing money.

How the eBay fee is built

Start with the total amount of the sale. That is the item price plus the shipping you charge plus the sales tax eBay collects. Everything percentage-based is applied to that number, not to the item price alone. The final value fee takes a slice up to $7,500 per item and 2.35 percent of anything above that, then the per-order fee is added once.

The formula in one line: fee = (item + shipping + tax) x category rate + per-order fee + promoted rate x total + international fee. The per-order fee is $0.30 when the total is $10.00 or less and $0.40 above it.

A worked example. You sell a used camera lens for $60, charge $8 shipping, and the buyer pays $5.40 in sales tax at 9 percent, so the total is $73.40. The final value fee at 13.6 percent is $9.98. The per-order fee is $0.40 because the order is above $10. Fees total $10.38. eBay remits the $5.40 tax to the state, so your payout is $73.40 minus $5.40 minus $10.38, which is $57.62. If the lens cost you $25 and the label and box cost $7, profit is $25.62 and the effective fee rate on the buyer's total is 14.1 percent. Note that the tax added $0.73 of fee you never see coming.

Common ways sellers use this page

  • Pricing a single item before listing. Work backwards from the profit you want, then read the break-even price the calculator returns and list above it.
  • Deciding on a store subscription. The 0.9 point discount needs roughly $2,440 of monthly sales to cover a $22 store, so low-volume sellers usually keep the free plan.
  • Testing a promoted listings rate. Raise the ad rate until profit per sale drops below your floor. The fee is charged only on sales that come from the ad, but the loss on those sales is real.
  • Sourcing at estate sales and bins. A $4 cost of goods looks safe until fees and shipping are subtracted. Run the number before you buy the lot, not after.
  • Comparing marketplaces. The same item usually nets a different amount on eBay, Etsy and a Shopify store, mostly because of how each platform treats shipping and tax in the fee base.

Auction, Buy It Now and the listing itself

The selling format does not change the percentage. An auction that ends at $82 and a Buy It Now listing at the same price pay identical fees, because the final value fee follows the closing price rather than the format. What the format changes is the closing price, and that is where the money is. Auction-style listings with a low opening bid attract watchers and can overshoot; fixed-price listings with a Best Offer option convert more reliably but tend to settle near the asking price. Neither route is cheaper on fees, so pick the one that gets the better price for the item you have.

Listing costs sit outside the sale entirely. US sellers get 250 free insertions a month on most accounts, and each insertion beyond that runs about $0.35 whether or not the item sells. A reseller who lists 400 items a month pays roughly $52 in insertion fees before a single sale, and a relisted item that failed to sell counts again. High-volume clothing sellers feel this most, since a $20 shirt that takes three attempts to move has already spent a dollar on listing fees.

Returns deserve their own line. When you refund a buyer, eBay credits the final value fee in proportion to the refund, but the per-order fee generally stays with eBay, and you still paid the outbound label. A $60 sale returned once costs the fixed fee and the postage even though the item comes back, which is why the margin on a 15 percent category can vanish after two returns in ten.

Used goods, grading and authentication

Resellers buying from thrift shops, estate auctions and liquidation pallets live on the same arithmetic with one extra risk: condition. A graded card in a sealed slab, a lens with a scratched coating, a jacket missing a button — each detail decides whether the listing earns a premium or triggers a return. Photograph the flaw, put measurements in the description and disclose the defect before the buyer discovers it. A returned parcel costs the fixed fee and the outbound label, and an item-not-as-described case can damage your seller standing, which pushes the percentage up six points if it slips below standard.

Authentication adds a second layer. Sneakers above $150, watches and designer handbags pass through eBay's inspectors, and that is why sneakers carry the low 8 percent rate with no per-order charge: the platform takes less cash in exchange for controlling the inspection. Handbags sit near 15 percent instead, so a $400 purse nets noticeably less than a $400 pair of shoes. Check the category before assuming a margin, and remember that three cheap pieces sold together usually pay three fixed charges rather than one, which is the quiet argument for bundling slow stock into a single lot.

Seasonality and price testing

Most resale money moves through the fourth quarter. December traffic lifts nearly every category, January brings a slump, and a listing that sits unsold through both has usually been mispriced rather than unseen. Test a figure by relisting at a different number and watching the watcher count for a week; that counter is the closest thing to demand data eBay hands you for free. Promoted standard placements bill only when a shopper clicks, while promoted advanced runs on a cost-per-sale model, and the two behave very differently on slow-moving stock. Handling time matters as well: a one-day dispatch badge wins the buy box on competitive listings even when your asking price is a dollar higher, and the fee percentage stays the same either way.

The form at the end of the year

eBay reports gross sales on a 1099-K, not profit. That figure is everything buyers paid, shipping and tax included, so a reseller whose real margin is 18 percent can look far more profitable on paper than the bank balance suggests. Keep receipts for inventory, postage, tape, mileage and any subscription; those deductions are what reconcile the two numbers. An accountant shown gross sales without them will estimate the tax bill far too high, and a seller who filed on the gross figure once tends not to repeat it.

Three listings, same 13.6 percent

A used drone at $240, with the buyer paying postage and a $150 cost, clears about $57. A cast iron skillet at $38 that cost $14 clears $18 when the buyer pays shipping, and $11 when you swallow a $7 label yourself. A sealed board game at $95 against a $30 cost clears $52, but only $47 once a 5 percent promoted rate applies. Identical percentage, three different outcomes, because a fixed charge and a postage decision weigh far more on a cheap item than on an expensive one. Collectors hunting diecast cars, vinyl records, sports cards or Lego sets usually sit in the middle of that range, where condition grading and a good photograph decide the closing bid.

Where the math usually goes wrong

The most common error is using an outdated fee model. Before managed payments, sellers paid a final value fee plus a separate processing charge of roughly 2.9 percent and thirty cents. Many calculators still do. If yours adds a processing line, it overstates costs by about three points and quietly talks you out of profitable inventory.

The second error is treating free shipping as fee-free. Folding $8 of postage into a $60 price means the fee is charged on $68 either way. You have not avoided the fee, you have only hidden it inside the item price, and the buyer now sees a higher sticker. Charge shipping separately or price it in deliberately, but do not assume one route is cheaper on fees. It is not.

The third is stacking optional fees without checking the base. Promoted listings, the 1.65 percent international fee and the Below Standard surcharge all apply to the same total that already includes tax and shipping, so they compound rather than sit side by side. A 5 percent ad rate on top of a 14 percent effective rate is not 19 percent of the item price, it is 19 percent of a total that includes tax, which lands closer to 20.7 percent of the item.

Finally, the calculator does not know about returns, insertion fees after your free allowance, or the cost of the box. Those are real. A 3 percent return rate looks harmless until you price the label on each parcel that comes back, and 100 extra listings a month at $0.35 is $35 that has to come from somewhere. Treat the output as the fee layer, then subtract the rest yourself.

Frequently asked questions

Why does eBay charge a final value fee on sales tax it collects for the state?

Because the fee is calculated on the total amount of the sale, which eBay defines as item price plus shipping plus sales tax. The tax never reaches your bank account, but the percentage is applied to it. On a 9 percent tax rate this adds about 1.2 points of fee on every taxed order.

Is a $10.00 order cheaper in fees than a $10.01 order?

Yes. The per-order fee is $0.30 at $10.00 or less and $0.40 above it, so a one cent price increase costs ten cents in fees. On very cheap items a 99 cent ending can sometimes beat a round number.

Do I still pay a PayPal or processing fee on top?

No. Managed payments folded processing into the final value fee. Any guide that adds 2.9 percent plus thirty cents is describing the pre-2021 structure.

Does a Basic Store subscription actually save money?

It lowers most categories from 13.6 to 12.7 percent. At about $22 a month you break even around $2,440 in monthly sales, so it pays only above that volume, and only if the included listings replace fees you were already paying.

How do I know if Promoted Listings is worth the ad rate?

Compare the ad rate to your margin. Five percent on a 20 percent margin keeps three quarters of the profit. Fifteen percent on a 12 percent margin turns each promoted sale into a loss. The ad fee is charged on the sale total, so it also eats the part of the price covering shipping and tax.

Why is my payout lower than the price minus 13.6 percent?

The fee is charged on shipping and tax as well as the item, the per-order fee is fixed, and any promoted or international fee uses the same base. On small orders the effective rate typically lands between 15 and 17 percent.
