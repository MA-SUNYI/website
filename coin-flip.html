A coin flip is the oldest fair randomiser there is, and the digital version has one advantage the physical coin does not: it cannot be biased by an uneven weight distribution or a practised thumb. Each flip is independent, so a run of five heads changes nothing about the sixth flip's odds.

The counter-intuitive part is how badly humans estimate what randomness looks like. Asked to write down twenty coin flips, most people produce far too few runs of four or five in a row, because streaks feel non-random. In reality, a run of five identical results turns up roughly once every sixteen sets of five flips. The tool showing five heads in a row is not a malfunction.

For decisions, the honest protocol is to declare what each outcome means before flipping. Best of three removes some of the sting of a single unlucky call, though it changes nothing mathematically about any individual flip. Long runs, if you want to test the tool, should land close to a 50/50 split once the sample gets large — small samples routinely look lopsided.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a computer coin flip really 50/50?

Yes. Each result is drawn independently with equal probability, unlike a physical coin where wear and weight can introduce a small bias.

Does a previous run of heads affect the next flip?

No. Flips are independent, so the probability remains 50% regardless of history. Believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy.

How many throws before results look balanced?

Several hundred at least. In twenty flips, a 60/40 split is entirely normal and does not indicate bias.

Best of three or single flip?

A single flip is fair by definition. Best of three reduces the impact of one unlucky call but does not change the probability of any individual throw.
