Self-Employment Tax Calculator

If you are paid on a 1099, nobody withholds Social Security and Medicare for you, and nobody matches it. You pay both halves yourself, then fund income tax on top. This calculator takes your net profit, any regular wages, and your filing status, and returns the SECA tax, the half-tax deduction, an income tax estimate and the amount to send in each quarter.

How the Self-Employment Tax Is Calculated

Three numbers drive everything: your net profit after business expenses, the Social Security wage base, and your filing status. The order matters, and skipping a step is the usual reason people either overpay estimates all year or get hit with a balance due in April.

First, profit is reduced to net earnings from self-employment at 92.35 percent. That factor exists because employees never pay tax on the employer half of payroll tax, and the reduced base gives the self-employed an equivalent treatment without a separate bookkeeping step.

Net earnings = Net profit × 0.9235
Social Security = 12.4% × min(Net earnings, $184,500 − W-2 wages)
Medicare = 2.9% × Net earnings
Additional Medicare = 0.9% × (Net earnings + wages − filing threshold, if positive)
SE tax = Social Security + Medicare + Additional Medicare
Deduction = SE tax ÷ 2

Worked example, single filer, no job, $80,000 of net freelance profit. Net earnings come to $80,000 × 0.9235 = $73,880. The Social Security piece is $73,880 × 12.4% = $9,161.12, Medicare is $73,880 × 2.9% = $2,142.52, and no additional Medicare applies because $73,880 sits well below $200,000. Total SECA tax is $11,303.64, and the deduction is $5,651.82. At an assumed 22 percent marginal rate, income tax adds ($80,000 − $5,651.82) × 22% = $16,356.60. The full set-aside is $27,660.24, or about $6,915 per quarter.

Notice what happened to the headline 15.3 percent. On the profit line the real burden is 14.13 percent, not 15.3, because the 92.35 percent base trims it. Then income tax lands on top. That is why a freelancer billing $80,000 keeps less than someone earning the same salary, who pays only 7.65 percent plus income tax.

What Different Profit Levels Actually Owe

The Social Security cap creates a visible kink in the curve. Below $184,500 of combined earnings the effective rate on profit holds near 14.13 percent. Above it, each extra dollar carries only the 2.9 percent Medicare charge, plus the surtax once income clears the filing threshold.

Three Mistakes That Distort the Number

Treating 15.3 percent as the whole tax bill. SECA is payroll tax. Federal income tax is separate and stacks on top, and state income tax may too. A freelancer who reserves 15.3 percent of every check and stops there will be short in April.

Assuming the half-tax deduction halves the SECA bill. It does not. The deduction reduces adjusted gross income, which lowers income tax by your marginal rate times half the SECA tax. On $11,303.64 of SECA tax at a 22 percent rate, the actual saving is about $1,243, not $5,651.

Assuming an LLC removes the tax. A single-member LLC files the same Schedule C and pays the same SECA tax. Electing S corporation status can reduce the base, but it brings payroll filings, reasonable salary rules and state fees, and the saving is often smaller than the pitch suggests.

Deadlines and Payment Mechanics

Self-employment tax is not paid with the April return alone. A calendar-year filer makes four estimated payments, generally due April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15 of the following year. Each payment covers income tax and SECA together, which is why the quarterly figure here combines both rather than isolating payroll tax.

Underpayment penalties are computed on the shortfall by quarter, so paying everything in January does not erase interest on the earlier quarters. If income is lumpy, the annualized income installment method can match payments to when the money actually arrived. The safe harbor worth knowing: paying at least 100 percent of last year's total tax, or 110 percent if adjusted gross income exceeded $150,000, avoids the penalty regardless of what this year's profit turns out to be.

Where This Estimate Stops

The income tax line uses a flat marginal rate you supply. Real 1040 arithmetic involves the standard or itemized deduction, the qualified business income deduction, credits and progressive brackets, so the estimate is a planning figure, not a return. It also ignores state tax, self-employed health insurance and retirement contributions, all of which reduce taxable income and therefore the real bill.

The SECA portion, by contrast, is close to exact for a given profit number, because it follows a fixed formula with a published wage base. Treat that part as reliable and the income tax line as a placeholder until you run the actual return.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the tax calculated on 92.35 percent of my profit instead of the full amount?

Schedule SE starts from 92.35 percent of net profit because you may deduct the employer half of Social Security and Medicare tax. Applying 15.3 percent to that reduced base produces the same dollars as deducting the employer share first, without a separate calculation.

Does the half-tax deduction reduce my self-employment tax?

No. It lowers taxable income on your 1040, so it saves income tax at your marginal rate. The 15.3 percent SECA bill itself does not change, which is why the calculator shows the deduction as its own line.

Do I still pay Social Security tax after my profit passes $184,500?

The 12.4 percent portion stops at $184,500 of combined wages and net self-employment earnings. Medicare at 2.9 percent continues on everything, and the extra 0.9 percent begins above $200,000 for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers.

Should I include W-2 wages in this calculator?

Yes, if you also hold a job. Wages consume the wage base first, so a $100,000 salary leaves only $84,500 of Social Security room for 1099 income. Entering zero overstates the tax on a side business.

How much should I set aside each quarter?

Start with the quarterly figure and add a cushion if revenue is uneven. Ten to fifteen percent above the calculated amount is common for freelance income, because a strong quarter raises the following quarter's required payment.

Does forming an LLC remove the self-employment tax?

A single-member LLC is disregarded federally, so the owner still files Schedule C and pays SECA tax. Only an S corporation election changes the base, and only the salary portion then carries payroll tax.
