How the Royalty Math Works

No major service publishes a fixed rate. Each month Spotify pools subscription and advertising revenue, hands roughly seventy percent of it to rights holders, and then divides that money by the total number of streams on the platform. Your share is your streams divided by everyone's streams, applied to the money pool. Because the pool and the denominator both move, the same thousand plays can be worth four dollars in December and three dollars in February. Every per-stream figure you read, including the ones in this tool, is a backward-looking average, which is why the output is presented as three bands.

The arithmetic for the mid band is deliberately plain:

  • Gross recording royalty = streams multiplied by the platform rate. 250,000 Spotify streams at $0.0038 gives $950.
  • Distribution step = gross multiplied by (1 minus distributor percentage). With a nine percent distributor, $950 becomes $864.50.
  • Collaborator step = that result multiplied by (1 minus the other rights holders percentage). Splitting a third with a producer turns $864.50 into $576.33.
  • Per thousand streams = your net divided by streams, times 1,000. Here it is $2.31, which is the number to compare against your statement.

The same 250,000 plays on Apple Music at $0.0085 gross $2,125 and net $1,933.75 after the distributor. On Tidal at $0.0125 the same plays gross $3,125 and net $2,843.75. Nothing about the music changed; only the divisor of each service's pool did. That single comparison explains most of the arguments artists have about which platform is best, and it is also the reason those arguments rarely settle: the platform with the highest rate has the fewest listeners to reach.

Why the Headline Rate Is Misleading

The most common mistake is treating a published per-stream rate as a promise. It is an average across millions of listeners you will never have, and it hides three things that decide your own number.

Free listeners pay a fraction of what subscribers pay

On Spotify a paid subscriber contributes several times more revenue than a free listener hearing ads. If your growth came from a playlist that skews toward students on the free tier, your effective rate will land near the bottom of the band even though your stream count looks healthy. There is no way to fix this directly, but it explains why two artists with identical monthly plays can differ by forty percent in payout.

Geography is a multiplier, not a detail

A play in a market with a two-dollar-a-month subscription contributes a small slice to the pool. When a campaign targets cheaper markets to grow numbers quickly, the stream count rises faster than the revenue. If a sponsor or a label is judging you by streams, that gap is worth flagging before a deal is signed.

The threshold quietly deletes small tracks

Spotify only pays recording royalties on a track once it passes 1,000 streams in a rolling twelve-month window. A catalogue of thirty album cuts sitting at six hundred plays each earns nothing at all, and the money is redistributed to tracks that did clear the line. This tool divides your net by the number of tracks you enter, and warns you when that average falls below two thousand, meaning a meaningful share of the release may not qualify.

What This Estimate Leaves Out

Three exclusions matter. Songwriting royalties are separate money: mechanical payments arrive through the Mechanical Licensing Collective and performance payments through a performing rights organization, and neither passes through your distributor. Failing to register with both is the most common reason an artist's total income is far below the pool's real size. Second, the tool does not model the publishing share of a cover or a sample clearance, which can pull a quarter of a track's income away permanently. Third, currency conversion and withholding on foreign royalties are ignored, and both can shave a few percent off a statement for non-US artists.

There is also a timing issue the arithmetic cannot solve. Streaming statements lag two to three months, a viral month is followed by a decay, and a playlist add that lifts one month may not renew. The honest way to use any of these numbers is to average four quarters before deciding that a lane is working.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does Spotify pay me less than three cents a stream when blogs say four?

The four-cent figure is a blended average for listeners in high-revenue countries on paid plans. If most of your plays come from free-tier listeners or cheaper markets, the pool behind each stream is smaller. The bands exist so you can see that gap instead of trusting one number.

Does my distributor really take a percentage?

Some do. Flat-fee services keep the royalty whole, while percentage services commonly keep nine to fifteen percent. The tool applies the distributor cut first, then any collaborator split, which mirrors how a statement reads.

What happens to plays before a track reaches 1,000 streams?

Spotify pays nothing on them until the track passes 1,000 streams inside a rolling twelve months. A catalogue of small tracks can sit just under the line and earn zero, with that money redistributed to tracks that qualified.

Why does Tidal pay more than Spotify for the same listener?

Tidal has no free tier and far fewer listeners, so each subscription is divided across fewer streams. The revenue share is similar; the divisor is not. That is also why a bigger service can show a lower average and still pay more in total.

Are songwriting royalties in this number?

No, this is the recording side only. Mechanical royalties flow through the MLC and performance royalties through your PRO on separate schedules. Artists who register with neither can lose a large share of the full pool.

Can I use this to predict next month if a track goes viral?

Treat the output as a range, not a forecast. A viral month lands at a premium-heavy rate and then decays, so the twelve-month average will be lower. Budget against annual net divided by twelve.
