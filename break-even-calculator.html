Break-even analysis identifies the sales volume at which revenue covers all costs. Below it the business loses money; above it, every additional unit contributes to profit. It is the simplest useful model in business planning.

The calculation needs two figures: fixed costs (rent, salaries, software) and contribution margin per unit (price minus variable cost). With $12,000 in monthly fixed costs and a $45 contribution per unit, break-even is 267 units. At a $30 contribution it is 400 units, and at $15 it is 800 — the contribution figure drives the answer far more than the fixed cost does.

The common error is classifying costs wrongly. A cost that varies with volume belongs in variable; one that does not belongs in fixed. Getting this wrong distorts the contribution margin and produces a break-even figure that looks precise and is wrong. When a cost is genuinely mixed, such as electricity with both a standing charge and usage, split it into its two components.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate break-even point?

Divide fixed costs by the contribution margin per unit (price minus variable cost). $12,000 fixed with $45 contribution gives 267 units.

What is contribution margin?

The amount each sale contributes toward fixed costs after variable costs are covered. It is price minus variable cost per unit.

Should break-even be calculated in units or revenue?

Both are useful. Units tell you what must be sold; revenue tells you whether that volume is realistic given market size.

Does break-even include the owner's salary?

It should, if the owner depends on the business for income. Excluding it produces a break-even figure that looks achievable but leaves the owner unpaid.
