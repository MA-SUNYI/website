Surrogacy Cost Calculator

Surrogacy quotes arrive as a stack of six-figure ranges that are impossible to compare because no two include the same lines. One agency bundles legal work, another leaves it out, and almost nobody mentions the second IVF cycle until you need it. This tool takes the published components apart, adds a contingency reserve you set yourself, and turns the total into a monthly saving target so the plan is attached to real cash flow rather than a single daunting number.

How the Surrogacy Cost Calculator Works

The total is built from seven grouped lines, each editable, so you can drop in a real quote instead of a national average:

Subtotal = surrogate compensation + allowance and expenses + agency fee + (IVF cost per cycle × cycles budgeted) + legal fees + insurance + escrow, psych and travel

Total = subtotal × (1 + contingency rate)

The contingency is applied to the whole subtotal rather than to a single line, because extra costs do not confine themselves to one category. A complication can trigger more compensation, more medical billing and more legal work at the same time.

Then the funding side: still to fund = total − already saved, and monthly set-aside = still to fund / months until transfer.

Worked example on the default values. Compensation $55,000 plus allowance $12,000 plus agency $30,000 plus two IVF cycles at $20,000 ($40,000) plus legal $12,000 plus insurance $15,000 plus escrow, psych and travel $8,000 gives a subtotal of $172,000. A 10% contingency adds $17,200 for a total of $189,200. If you have $40,000 saved and 18 months before a planned transfer, the gap is $149,200, which means setting aside about $8,289 per month.

Change one thing and the arithmetic shifts hard. Drop to one IVF cycle and the subtotal falls by $20,000 to $152,000, and the total to $167,200. Cut the agency fee to a $10,000 matching-only arrangement and you save a further $20,000 on the subtotal, but the case management, screening and escrow work still has to be bought somewhere, which is why the allowance line usually grows in that scenario. Notice how the contingency scales with everything: a 5% reserve on the default plan is $8,600, a 20% reserve is $34,400. That single input moves the total by more than the difference between one and two IVF cycles.

Why the Advertised Price and the Invoice Never Match

Compensation escalates with every clinical event. Base compensation covers a straightforward singleton pregnancy. Twins, a C-section, invasive testing or prescribed bed rest commonly add thousands in contractual add-ons that were always in the agreement but never in the headline quote.

Two IVF cycles is a budget, not a pessimism. A single transfer frequently does not result in a clinical pregnancy, and medication plus monitoring follows every attempt. Assuming success on the first transfer is the single most common way a plan runs $20,000 to $40,000 short.

Insurance is a cliff, not a slope. If the surrogate's existing plan excludes surrogacy, you buy a policy for the year, and complications sit on top of the premium. Two otherwise identical arrangements can differ by $25,000 purely on the insurance line.

Fees are earned at different moments. Agency fees are often earned on match, legal fees on signing, compensation at milestones. If the arrangement ends partway, the refundable and non-refundable split decides how much of the money is gone. Read the escrow rules before you fund the account, not after.

Reserve is not padding. A 10% reserve on this kind of plan is roughly the cost of one extra cycle. Plans that carry no reserve are effectively betting there is no second attempt and no complication.

What this tool cannot do: it does not evaluate agencies, check state law on parentage, or tell you which clinic will work. Parentage recognition varies enormously between states, and some arrangements that are cheap to start are expensive to complete legally. Use the numbers here to compare quotes on equal footing, then spend the legal and medical questions on professionals licensed where you live.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many IVF cycles should I budget into a surrogacy plan?

More than one. A single transfer is often budgeted as one cycle, but many intended parents need two or three attempts, and each adds $15,000 to $30,000 with medication and monitoring. Budget two and treat a third as contingency.

Why is the surrogate's health insurance the line most likely to blow up?

A policy that already covers surrogacy leaves only deductibles and copays. If a surrogacy-friendly plan must be bought, it commonly runs $15,000 to $30,000 for the year, with complications, a hospital stay or NICU charges on top. Get the exclusions in writing.

Is independent matching genuinely cheaper than using an agency?

Often on the invoice, rarely in total risk. The screening, evaluation, escrow and case management still get paid for separately, and legal complexity tends to rise when nobody is managing the timeline. Compare the all-in number.

What happens to the money if the arrangement falls through?

Depends on the contract and escrow rules. Compensation is usually released in milestones, so a failed transfer costs a portion. Ask every vendor which fees are refundable and which are earned on signing.

Do I need to add income tax to the surrogacy figure?

Possibly. Some medical expenses may qualify for an itemised deduction, and reimbursed compensation can be taxable to the surrogate. Treat the total here as a pre-tax cash requirement and confirm treatment with a tax professional.

How does this compare with the cost of a routine unassisted birth?

Not comparable. An insured hospital delivery is commonly a few thousand dollars out of pocket; surrogacy involves compensation, agency work, fertility medicine and two sets of counsel, which is why it lands in six figures. Itemising it is the only way to see where.
