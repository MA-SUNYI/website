Time zone conversion looks like simple addition until you involve daylight saving, half-hour offsets or a meeting that spans three regions. The converter takes a time in one zone and returns the equivalent in another, accounting for the offset in effect on that date.

Offsets are not integers of an hour everywhere. India is UTC+5:30, Nepal is UTC+5:45, and parts of Australia are UTC+9:30. Daylight saving adds another layer: it starts and ends on different dates in the northern and southern hemispheres, and a few regions have abolished it or shifted their rules in recent years. A conversion that ignores the date is wrong twice a year for about half the world.

For scheduling, always convert to the participants' local times before agreeing, and state the time zone explicitly in the invitation. '3 pm' without a zone is the single most common cause of missed international meetings, and the error is invisible until someone joins an hour late.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I schedule a meeting across time zones?

Convert the proposed time into each participant's local zone and confirm in writing with the zone name attached. Avoid quoting a bare time without an offset.

Why is India not on a whole-hour offset?

India uses UTC+5:30 to align its single national time zone with the country's geography. Several other countries use fractional offsets for similar reasons.

Does the converter handle daylight saving?

It applies the offset in effect on the date you enter, which differs from the offset six months earlier in most DST regions.

What is UTC and why use it as a base?

UTC is the global reference time with no offsets or daylight saving. Converting through UTC avoids ambiguity when several zones are involved.
