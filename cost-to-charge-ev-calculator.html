Cost to Charge an EV Calculator

The sticker price of a charge is rarely the price you pay. You pay for the kilowatt-hours that leave the meter, not the ones that land in the pack, and the miles you get from those kilowatt-hours fall faster than most drivers expect at highway speed, in cold weather, or as the battery ages. This tool converts a pack size, a charge added and a driving distance into cost per charge, per 100 miles, per mile and per year, at three different prices so you can see what switching from the driveway to a fast charger actually costs.

How the cost is worked out

Two conversions do all the work. The first is from battery percentage to energy: kilowatt-hours added to the pack equals usable capacity times the change in state of charge, divided by 100. The second is from pack energy to meter energy, which is where charging loss enters. Meter energy equals pack energy divided by one minus the loss rate expressed as a decimal. Every dollar in the table is charged on the meter figure, because that is what the utility or the network bills.

From there, cost per 100 miles is meter energy per session divided by the miles that session delivers, then multiplied by 100. Miles per session equals pack kilowatt-hours added times miles per kWh. Cost per mile is simply cost per hundred divided by 100, and the annual figure scales the per-mile number by yearly distance.

A worked example. A 75 kWh usable battery charged from 10 percent to 90 percent takes on 60 kWh in the pack. At 9 percent charging loss the meter supplies 60 divided by 0.91, about 65.93 kWh. At 3.2 miles per kWh that session moves 192 miles. At the US average residential price of about 17 cents per kWh, the charge costs about $11.21, which is $0.058 per mile or $5.84 per 100 miles. The same 60 kWh at a 45 cent fast charger costs about $29.67 for the same 192 miles, or $15.45 per 100 miles.

The annual line blends the two, using the share you charge away from home at the public level 2 price, since most drivers who use a fast charger do so on trips rather than as routine. Raising the loss rate, lowering miles per kWh, or shifting more charging to public stations all push the annual number up, and the tool shows the sensitivity immediately.

A fair comparison with gasoline has to use the same distance. Take a 12,000 mile year, 3.2 miles per kWh, and a 17 cent home rate with 20 percent of charging at a 28 cent public station. The blended figure comes out near $791 a year, about $66 a month, because 12,000 miles needs roughly 4,121 kWh at the meter once the 9 percent charge loss is counted. A 30 mpg car on the same 12,000 miles burns 400 gallons, which at $3.10 a gallon is $1,240. The gap is real, but it shrinks quickly if the home rate is 30 cents, if winter efficiency drops to 2.4 miles per kWh, or if most charging happens at a fast charger.

Price changes fast and by region, so the planner is only as good as the per-kWh number you type. Hawaii can sit above 45 cents, while parts of Louisiana and Washington run near 11 cents, and two neighboring utilities can differ by 6 cents on the same street. The honest approach is to take the energy charge plus the delivery charge from your own bill, divide by total kilowatt-hours used, and add any per-kWh riders. Rebates and free-charging perks at work are real but should be modelled separately, because they tend to end.

Two habits change the arithmetic more than any app. The first is setting the car to stop at 80 percent on a daily basis, which keeps the majority of sessions inside the fast part of the charging curve and avoids paying for slow kilowatt-hours. The second is scheduling the home session to start after the overnight window opens rather than when the car is parked, so a flat 17 cent tariff becomes a 9 cent one for the same energy. Neither changes how you drive, and together they can move the yearly figure by a third.
