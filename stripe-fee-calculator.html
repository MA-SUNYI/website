Stripe Fee Calculator

Stripe publishes one headline number, 2.9% + $0.30, and the rest of the rate card sits further down a pricing page most merchants never finish reading. What you actually pay depends on how the money comes in: a tapped card, a typed card number, a bank debit or a card issued abroad each carries its own price. This tool takes the amount and the rail, applies the 2026 US standard rates, and shows the deduction, your net payout, the effective rate you really paid, and the sum to quote if you need a fixed figure to land in your account.

How the fee is built

Every Stripe charge is a percentage of the amount plus a fixed component, and the add-ons are further percentage points rather than their own line items on your dashboard. The general form is:

Fee = Amount x percentage rate + fixed fee

A $250 online card payment costs 250 x 2.9% + $0.30 = $7.25 + $0.30 = $7.55. You bank $242.45, an effective rate of 3.02% against the quoted 2.9%. The difference is the fixed component carried across one ticket. On a $10 charge the same rate costs $0.59, which is 5.9% of the money, and on a $1,000 bill it costs $29.30, or 2.93%.

Working backwards is where merchants lose money. To net a fixed sum you cannot bolt the percentage onto your price, because Stripe deducts from the higher figure too. Divide by one minus the rate and put the fixed component back first:

Amount to charge = (Target net + fixed fee) / (1 - percentage rate)

To end up with $1,000 from a domestic card charge: ($1,000 + $0.30) / 0.971 = $1,030.18. Stripe takes $30.18 and your $1,000 lands. Bolting 2.9% and 30 cents onto $1,000 gives $1,029.30, a gap of $0.87 every time you quote that way.

That single switch beats any negotiation over a tenth of a percentage point, though it only works where the customer accepts a bank debit and slower settlement.

The add-ons most merchants trigger quietly

International cards. A card issued abroad adds 1.5 percentage points. On a $99 download sold to a Canadian buyer the deduction is $4.66 rather than $3.17, and that is the good case where the price is already in dollars.

Currency conversion. If the buyer pays in euros and you settle in dollars, another point goes on top, so a cross-border sale with conversion costs 5.4% + $0.30. That is $5.70 on a $100 sale, nearly double the domestic rate.

Instant Payouts. Standard payouts in one to two business days are free. Moving the balance to a debit card within minutes costs 1.5% with a $0.50 floor, so pulling $1,000 forward for a weekend costs $15, and pulling $100 costs $1.50, which is 1.5% of a small balance for a day or two of earlier access.

Disputes. Stripe bills $15 for every dispute opened, win or lose. Contesting one by hand adds another $15, returned only if you win. Losing also gives back the sale amount, so a $120 chargeback can cost $150 plus the inventory. Smart Disputes, which assembles the evidence, takes 30% of the disputed amount on the disputes it wins.

Refunds, subscriptions and where this model stops

A refund returns the money to the customer but not the deduction to you. Refunding a $200 charge leaves you $6.10 down with nothing to show for it, which is why heavy send-back volumes have to be priced into the margin rather than treated as an occasional annoyance. Refunding a fraction of a charge works the same way: the deduction stands as calculated on the original charge.

Two Stripe products stack on top of the payment fee. Billing, which handles subscriptions and recurring bills, adds 0.7% of Billing volume on pay-as-you-go terms. Stripe Tax adds 0.5% per transaction where it calculates and files sales tax. A subscription business using both is really paying 4.1% + $0.30 on domestic card volume, not 2.9%, so a 20% margin loses a fifth of a point more than the payment page suggests.

The calculator uses the standard US rate card and assumes one charge per payment. Custom and IC+ pricing for high volume is not modelled, nor are network fees on some dispute outcomes, so treat the output as a planning estimate and your processor's report as the record.

Why the effective rate is the number to track

Most merchants compare processors on the headline percentage and then wonder why the deposit does not match. The recurring mistake is treating 2.9% as a cost control problem when the fixed component, the mix of payment methods and the refund rate are doing the damage. A store on 2.9% + $0.30 with an $18 average order pays 4.57% of revenue in processing alone, and a 4% refund rate pushes that past 4.7%, so switching processors to save 0.2 points recovers far less than lifting the average order by $5 or moving bills beyond $625 to bank debit.

Run the arithmetic on your own average ticket before trusting any rate comparison, from any provider, including this page.
