Airbnb Income Calculator

Most hosting projections stop at nightly rate times booked nights, which is the one number that never reaches your bank account. This calculator starts from room revenue and cleaning charges, takes out the platform fee and your cleaning bill, then subtracts the fixed monthly costs that run whether a guest shows up or not. You get net annual and monthly income plus the occupancy rate you need just to break even, which is usually a lot higher than hosts expect.

How the Airbnb Income Calculator Works

Three layers sit between the nightly rate and your net income: how many nights actually sell, what the platform keeps, and what it costs to run the place.

Booked nights and stays. Occupancy applies to the nights you make available, not to 365 by default. A property you block for two weeks of personal use offers 351 nights, and at 65 percent that is 228 booked nights, not 237. Stays are booked nights divided by average length of stay, and that quotient drives how many cleanings you pay for.

Revenue. Gross revenue = (nightly rate × booked nights) + (cleaning fee × number of stays)

Platform and management fees. Airbnb moved to a single host-only service fee, roughly 15.5 percent of the booking subtotal in most markets as of late 2026, replacing the older split of about 3 percent from the host and 14 percent from the guest. Firms running older 3 percent assumptions are quoting a fee structure that no longer exists for most listings. The field here is editable because the rate varies by market and by account, and because VRBO and Booking.com charge differently.

Costs. Cleaning cost per stay is a real cash cost, and it is not offset by the cleaning fee unless you charge at least what you pay plus the platform fee on that charge. Fixed monthly costs cover the mortgage or rent, insurance, utilities, internet, supplies and software; multiply by 12 for the annual figure.

Net income = gross revenue − platform fee − management fee − cleaning costs − annual fixed costs

Worked example. A one-bedroom at $180 a night, 365 nights available, 65 percent occupancy, a 15.5 percent platform fee, $150 cleaning fee charged and $180 cleaning cost, four-night average stay and $2,200 a month in fixed costs. Booked nights are 237.25 and stays are 59.3. Room revenue is $42,705 and cleaning revenue $8,897, for gross revenue of $51,602. The platform keeps $7,998 and cleaning costs $10,676, which is $1,779 more than guests paid for cleaning. Fixed costs are $26,400. Net income is $6,527 a year, or $544 a month, and break-even occupancy is 52.1 percent.

Notice how little of that $51,602 survives. The gap between a 52 percent break-even and a 65 percent actual is only 13 occupancy points, and a bad winter can erase it entirely.

Where Hosts Get the Math Wrong

Using revenue from a good month. Peak-season nightly rates and occupancy are not annual averages. Take twelve months of data from comparable listings, then cut the number by 10 percent for the optimism that every host projects onto their own property.

Forgetting that furnishing is a recurring cost. A short-term rental needs furniture, linens, kitchen gear and electronics replaced every few years, and guests break things. Setting aside 3 to 5 percent of gross revenue for replacement and damage is realistic; skipping it produces a profit figure that only exists on paper.

Treating cleaning as free money. If you charge $150 and pay $180 plus 15.5 percent on the charge, you lose money on every stay. Cleaning is a pass-through cost at best. Many experienced hosts fold cleaning into the nightly rate instead, which removes the fee from the platform's cut and encourages longer stays.

Ignoring regulation until after the purchase. Many US cities cap non-owner-occupied nights, require a permit, or ban short-term rentals in residential zones outright, and HOAs add their own rules. A property that models at 70 percent occupancy under a 90-night annual cap is a different investment entirely, and the cap is not negotiable.

Assuming the mortgage is the only fixed cost. Insurance for a rental is higher than for an owner-occupied home, utilities run higher with guests, and software, supplies and permits are small lines that add up to a few thousand a year. Those belong in the fixed monthly field, not in a footnote.

Frequently Asked Questions

What occupancy rate is realistic for a first year on Airbnb?

Budget 45 to 55 percent for a brand-new listing in a competitive market, even if comparable listings show 65 percent. A new listing has no reviews and no ranking history, and reaching mature occupancy usually takes two to four months. Modeling year one at the mature rate is the most common way hosts overpay.

Why does my payout look smaller than nightly rate times nights booked?

Because the platform fee, the cleaning you collect and the cleaning you pay move in different directions. The fee is charged on room revenue plus the cleaning fee, while your cleaning bill is separate, and the gap between those two cleaning numbers is often a quiet annual loss.

Are cleaning fees profitable for hosts?

Only when the fee you charge exceeds what the cleaner charges plus the platform fee on that charge. On a 15.5 percent fee, a $150 cleaning charge nets about $127, so a $150 bill loses roughly $23 per stay. Short stays multiply that fastest.

What does the average length of stay change in the math?

It sets the number of turnovers. The same 237 booked nights are 59 stays at four nights or 30 stays at eight nights, and every extra turnover adds a cleaning cost plus supplies and wear. Longer stays usually produce more net income at the same occupancy.

Does this calculator include lodging tax and insurance?

Lodging tax is excluded because hosts collect and remit it on top of the nightly rate, so it is not your revenue. Short-term rental insurance, permits and licensing are excluded as well; add the annual policy and permit fees to the fixed monthly figure.

Is short-term renting still worth it compared with a long-term lease?

Only when gross short-term revenue beats twelve months of market rent by a wide margin. Furnishing, utilities, supplies, platform fees and management commonly consume 45 to 60 percent of gross short-term revenue, so a thin premium over annual rent disappears quickly.
