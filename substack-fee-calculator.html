Substack Fee Calculator

Substack advertises a flat 10% cut, and that number is honest as far as it goes. What it leaves out is Stripe, which handles every card charge and takes 2.9% plus 30 cents, and Stripe Billing, which adds a recurring payment fee on top. For a $5 monthly tier the three charges together run about 19% of what the reader pays. This calculator runs your price, billing period and subscriber count through all three lines and shows the net that actually reaches your bank account.

How the Substack Fee Calculator Works

Three deductions hit every paid reader, in this order:

  • Platform cut — 10% of the gross. Taken on what the reader pays, before any card costs. No tier lowers it and volume buys no discount.
  • Card processing — 2.9% plus $0.30 on a typical US card. Charged per transaction, so a monthly plan triggers it twelve times a year and an annual plan once.
  • Stripe Billing cut — 0.7% on each renewal. It covers subscription management and sits on its own dashboard line, which is why writers quote "just 10% plus Stripe" and lowball themselves.

For a single charge of P dollars:

Net per charge = P − (0.10 × P) − (0.029 × P + 0.30) − (0.007 × P)

The sequence matters. Card networks do not skim a discounted remainder; it bills on the gross amount charged to the card. So the percentages stack additively: 10% + 2.9% + 0.7% = 13.6% of the price, plus a flat $0.30.

Worked example: a $6 monthly tier with 100 paid subscribers. Each charge collects $6.00. Substack takes $0.60, card processing takes $0.174 + $0.30 = $0.474, and billing takes $0.042. All deductions come to $1.116 per reader, 18.6% of what they paid, leaving $4.884 each. Across 100 subscribers that is $600 gross and $488.40 net, or $5,860.80 a year.

Move that entry to $8: deductions become $0.80 + $0.532 + $0.056 = $1.388, so net per subscriber is $6.612 and 100 subscribers clear $661.20 a month. The fixed $0.30 has fallen from 5.0% of the price to 3.75%.

What a Newsletter Actually Keeps: Three Realistic Sizes

Percentages are abstract, so here are three publications run end to end at a $7 monthly tier, where each charge nets $7 − $0.70 − $0.503 − $0.049 = $5.748, an effective rate of 17.9%.

  • New publication, 40 paid readers. Gross $280, net $229.92 a month. Fees cost $50.08, but the platform is also providing hosting and email delivery for that amount, which is roughly what a basic email tool charges on its own.
  • Growing publication, 180 paid readers. Gross $1,260, net $1,034.64. The fee total crosses $225 a month here, enough that a writer with an established list starts comparing email provider quotes.
  • Established publication, 900 paid readers. Gross $6,300, net $5,173.20. At this size the platform fee is the single largest recurring cost after the writer's own time, and the annual-plan mix matters more than any tier tweak.

Same 900 readers on annual billing at $70 a year, collecting about $5,250 a month: each charge nets $70 − $7.00 − $2.33 − $0.49 = $60.18, or $5.015 a month, so net is $4,513.50 a month and the whole year of $70 charges lands in the bank up front. The annual route keeps slightly less in the example because the hypothetical price offers two months free; at the same annual price the annual route wins. Which one is better depends entirely on what discount you attach to the yearly plan.

Common Usage

  • See what a paid tier pays out before you launch it, not after the first statement.
  • Decide where to land between $4 and $8 for an entry tier.
  • Compare a $6 monthly plan with a $60 annual plan on net dollars, not on the discount percentage.
  • Project take-home as the paying audience grows or slips.
  • Set a revenue target and work backwards: at 18.6% fees, $1,000 net a month needs about 205 paid subscribers on a $6 tier ($1,230 gross).

Why the Headline Number Barely Matters

Tweaking percentages feels controllable, so writers spend weeks on it. But consider two newsletters of 5,000 free readers, both at $6 a month. Newsletter A converts 2% and keeps $4.88 per subscriber, for 100 paid readers and $488 a month. Newsletter B converts 4% and keeps the same amount per reader, for 200 paid readers and $976 a month. Doubling conversion doubled income; the fee structure never moved. Getting from 18.6% down to 15.1% by switching to annual billing shifts the figure in the annual column by a few tenths of a point and brings a year of cash forward, but it cannot add readers.

What deductions do change is your floor. Marginal delivery expense for a text newsletter sits near zero, so nearly every paying reader stays profitable. The real constraint is time. A tier priced at $4 needs 25% more readers than a $5 tier for the same net revenue, and if your writing hours are the bottleneck, under-pricing quietly raises your cost per dollar earned.

Four Habits That Skew Revenue Projections

Missing the billing cut. Stripe Billing is not inside the processing figure shown by default. A writer modeling 13% total costs on a $10 tier will find payouts about 0.7% lighter than expected every month, which adds up across a list of hundreds.

Applying Stripe to the post-Substack amount. Some spreadsheets compute the platform cut first and then Stripe on the remainder. Real charges are computed on the gross. On a $10 tier the difference is about three cents per reader, trivial per sale and noticeable at scale.

Assuming overseas readers cost the same. This page models the standard US card rate. Many subscribers pay with cards issued elsewhere, in other currencies, which brings currency conversion and higher regional rates. Your effective take-home can drop several points if your audience is mostly outside the US.

Treating gross MRR as income. $1,000 of monthly subscription revenue yields roughly $814 to $866, depending on price and billing period. Budgeting on the gross number is how writers discover their annual plan is short.

What the Math Leaves Out

Platform and payment costs only are modeled. Excluded: federal and state income tax and self-employment tax on the net amount, which for most US writers is several times larger than the fee stack. It also excludes currency conversion, failed payments and chargebacks, refunds, and any spend on promotion or paid recommendations. Substack can change its rate or Stripe's terms, so confirm the current schedule in your publication settings before setting prices. Nothing here is tax advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Substack charge 10% on monthly and annual plans the same way?

Yes. The platform fee has no tier and no volume discount, so a $6 monthly plan and a $60 annual plan each pay $0.60 to Substack. The difference comes from Stripe, where the 30 cent fixed part is charged per transaction.

What is the extra 0.7% Stripe billing fee on my payouts?

Stripe Billing levies it on subscriptions that bill again without a new checkout. It is separate from the 2.9% plus 30 cents card processing and sits on a different line in the dashboard, which is why it is the most commonly omitted cost in Substack revenue models.

How much does a writer keep from a $50 annual subscription?

About $43.05. Platform $5.00, card processing $1.75, billing $0.35, for $7.10 total and a 14.2% effective rate. The same annual price split into twelve $4.17 payments would cost about 16.5%.

Do free subscribers and free posts cost me anything?

No. Fees apply only to paid revenue. Free posts, the list and delivery carry no platform charge, which is why conversion rate moves net income far more than a fee point.

Should I price my lowest tier at $4 or $5 a month?

Above $5 if you can. A $4 tier loses 21.1% of each charge to fees against 19.6% at $5, but the bigger effect is in net pay: $3.16 a month per reader at $4 versus $4.02 at $5. The 30 cent fixed part shrinks as a share, so a 25% price increase buys a 27% increase in net.

Is a self-hosted newsletter cheaper than Substack?

Sometimes, but not because of the 10%. Substack bundles hosting, email delivery and checkout. Self-hosting replaces the platform fee with an email provider bill that grows with list size, plus your own Stripe costs and deliverability work. Compare net dollars at your real list size.
