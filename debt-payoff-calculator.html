The payoff calculator answers a single question with more precision than intuition allows: given a balance, a rate and a fixed payment, how many months until zero, and how much interest will that cost in total?

The formula uses a logarithm because the balance decays geometrically. For a $10,000 balance at 18% APR with a $400 monthly payment, the monthly rate is 1.5%, and the payoff comes out at roughly 32 months with about $2,600 in interest. Drop the payment to $250 and the term stretches beyond 60 months, with interest climbing past $6,000. The relationship is not linear: halving the payment more than doubles the cost.

There is a hard floor to be aware of. If the monthly payment is less than the monthly interest charge — $10,000 at 18% accrues about $150 a month — the balance never falls, and no number of months will clear it. Any payoff figure in that situation is meaningless, and the payment has to rise above the interest line first.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is debt payoff time calculated?

Using the amortisation formula solved for the number of periods: n = -ln(1 - r×P/A) ÷ ln(1+r), where r is the monthly rate, P the balance and A the payment.

What if my payment is smaller than the monthly interest?

The balance grows instead of shrinking, and it will never be paid off at that payment level. The payment has to exceed the monthly interest charge before any progress is possible.

Does a larger payment always shorten the term proportionally?

No. The relationship is non-linear, and the effect is strongest at the start. Doubling a payment more than halves the interest paid.

Should I pay off debt or invest?

Compare the interest rate on the debt with the expected after-tax return on the investment. Guaranteed savings at 20% APR are hard for any investment to beat with certainty.
