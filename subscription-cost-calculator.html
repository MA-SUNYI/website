Subscription Cost Calculator

Subscriptions are priced to be forgettable: a few dollars a month, charged automatically, never reviewed. Individually they are trivial. Added together they are often the third largest line in a household budget, behind housing and food. This calculator normalizes every charge to a monthly figure, including the annual plans that never show up in a monthly scan, then shows the yearly total, the daily cost and what the same money would be worth if it had been invested instead.

Result:

Yearly plans are divided by 12, quarterly by 3, weekly multiplied by 52 then divided by 12. The invested comparison assumes the monthly total is contributed at the start of each period at a constant annual return.

How the Subscription Cost Calculator Works

Every billing cycle is converted to a monthly equivalent first, because adding a 15.99 monthly charge to a 119.99 annual charge directly produces a meaningless number. The conversion is:

Monthly equivalent = price / 12 for annual, price / 3 for quarterly, price x 52 / 12 for weekly, unchanged for monthly

Then total monthly = sum of all monthly equivalents, and annual = monthly x 12. The daily figure divides the monthly total by 30.44, the average number of days in a month, which is more accurate across a year than dividing by 30. The invested comparison uses the future value of a series of monthly contributions:

FV = monthly x ((1 + r/12)^(12n) - 1) / (r/12)

Worked example, five services: 15.99 and 9.99 billed monthly, 8.00 billed monthly, one plan at 59.99 a year and one at 199 a year. The monthly charges add to 33.98. The annual plans convert to 4.9992 and 16.5833, so the monthly equivalent is 55.56 and the year costs 666.75. Over five years that is 3,333.75 in cash. Invested at 7% instead, the same 55.56 a month grows to roughly 3,978, about 644 dollars more than the subscriptions cost.

That 644 dollar gap is not an argument for cancelling everything. It is the correct size of the trade: about 13% of the five-year spend, not the tripled number people often imagine. The honest question is whether those five services delivered 3,333 dollars of value, and the cost per use column is what answers it.

Why the Total Always Surprises People

Annual plans are invisible in a monthly scan. A 119.99 a year plan is 10 dollars a month, but it appears as a single charge once, in a month you were not looking. Roughly a third of the gap between what people guess they spend and what they actually spend comes from annual renewals.

Free trials convert without a notification you will notice. The charge is small, the email goes to a promotions tab, and three months later it is part of the baseline. Treat the trial end date as a calendar event, not a memory test.

Price increases are applied silently. Streaming services have raised prices repeatedly, usually by a dollar or two, and the increase arrives without a new decision from you. A budget built two years ago understates today's total by 15 to 25 percent.

Per-month framing hides per-use cost. A 20 dollar service used daily is cheap; the same 20 dollars used twice a month is not. Subscription pricing is deliberately expressed in a unit that discourages that division.

Where This Estimate Stops

The calculator totals what you enter, so its accuracy depends entirely on the audit behind it. It does not read your bank account, detect forgotten charges or know about subscriptions billed through an app store under a different merchant name. It also treats every charge as a flat amount: usage-based bills, annual price escalators and promotional rates that expire after six or twelve months will drift from the result. Currency is not converted, so keep every price in the same currency before entering it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does the average US household spend on subscriptions?

Self-reported surveys land around 60 to 90 dollars a month, while audits of actual bank and card statements usually find 130 to 220 dollars. The gap is almost never dishonesty; it is annual renewals, app store charges and free trials that converted quietly.

Should a yearly subscription be divided by 12 or by 52?

By 12 to get a monthly equivalent, because the bill arrives once a year and you are spreading that single charge across twelve months. Dividing by 52 only makes sense for something you actually want to express per week. Mixing the two is the most common way people undercount their total.

My total looks too low compared with my bank statement. What am I missing?

Usually four things: annual renewals that do not appear in a monthly scan, subscriptions billed through Apple or Google rather than directly, charges split with family members where the plan sits in someone else's name, and price increases that quietly raised a bill you set up years ago.

Is it worth cancelling a service I only use twice a month?

Compare cost per use, not cost per month. A 16 dollar service used twice costs 8 dollars a session, which may still be cheaper than renting the same title individually. A 16 dollar service used once every two months costs 32 dollars a session, and that is the number that should decide it.

How do I count a subscription I share with family?

Count only your share of the payment, and note who holds the card. If you pay the full 25 dollar family plan and three people reimburse you, your real cost is the net amount after those transfers, not the headline price.

What return should I use in the invested instead comparison?

Seven percent a year is the long-run nominal figure people usually cite for a broad US stock index. It is an illustration of opportunity cost, not a promise: real returns vary widely and can be negative over a five-year window. The point of the comparison is the size of the number, not its precision.
