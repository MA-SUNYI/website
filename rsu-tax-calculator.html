RSU Tax Calculator

Restricted stock units are taxed the moment they vest, not the day they are granted. How the RSU Tax Calculator Works

Every vesting event has two parts and they are taxed differently. The first is ordinary income: shares multiplied by the fair market value on the vest date. That amount is added to your W-2 wages and taxed at ordinary rates plus FICA. The second is a capital gain: whatever the price does after vesting, taxed only when you sell. Keeping the two apart is the whole point, because people routinely mix them up and conclude they were taxed twice.

The net-share formula is short. Taxable income equals shares times vest price. Total withholding rate equals federal plus FICA plus state. Withheld value equals taxable income times that rate, and net shares equal shares times one minus the withholding rate. The tax gap is the difference between withholding and what you would owe at your true marginal rate.

Run the numbers on a typical grant. 400 shares vest at $85, so the vest value is $34,000. Withholding is 22 percent federal, 7.65 percent FICA and 5 percent state, or 34.65 percent total, which is $11,781. Net shares come to 261.4, and because brokers withhold whole shares you would receive 261 shares with a small cash residual. Now suppose your real marginal federal rate is 32 percent, not 22. The true federal tax on $34,000 is $10,880, but payroll withheld $7,480. That $3,400 shortfall lands on your April return, which is why a big vest often turns into an unexpected payment rather than a refund.

The calculator also handles the second leg. If you sell those 261 shares three months later at $95, the proceeds are $24,795. Your basis is $22,185, the vest price times those shares, so the gain is $2,610 and it is taxed at short-term rates. Hold past twelve months and the same gain is taxed at long-term rates, often 15 percent instead of 35 percent or more.

The Mistake That Costs the Most

The expensive error is not selling at vest, or holding too long, or picking the wrong bracket. It is failing to sell enough shares to cover the tax. When a vest delivers 261 shares you own a concentrated position in the company that also pays your salary, and the same 261 shares are quietly standing in for money you owe the IRS. If the stock drops 30 percent between vest and filing, the tax bill does not drop with it, because it was calculated on the vest price. You still owe tax on value that no longer exists.

The second most common error is double counting. People see the withholding on their pay stub, see the vested shares in their brokerage account, and assume they were taxed on the full grant, then pay again. They were not. Only the withheld portion was taxed, and the shares you received already carry the vest price as basis.

There are limits worth naming. This calculator does not know your other income, deductions, or the Alternative Minimum Tax, and it treats FICA as applying in full. Above the Social Security wage base, roughly $184,500 for 2026, the 6.2 percent portion stops, so the FICA rate drops to 1.45 percent on the excess. Employers often handle that automatically, but the rate you enter here is yours to confirm. State tax also varies from zero in Texas and Washington to double digits in California, and some states tax RSU income where you worked, not where you live. Treat the output as a planning estimate and confirm the final number with payroll or a tax preparer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why did only part of my grant land in my brokerage account? Only part of it ever became yours in economic terms. The withheld shares were sold to pay the employer's share of payroll tax and your income tax. The cash shows up on your W-2 as wages, and the withholding appears as tax already paid, so nothing is missing, it simply went to the IRS and your state instead of your account.

Is it better to sell my RSUs the day they vest? Most financial planners say yes for anyone whose paycheck already depends on the same company. Holding is a deliberate bet that this one stock will beat a diversified portfolio, and it doubles your exposure if the company struggles. The counterargument is that holding lets you reach long-term rates, but the tax saved is usually smaller than the concentration risk taken.

My grant vests over four years, so do I need to redo this every year? Yes, and the price will differ each time. A vest at $40 and one at $90 create two separate cost bases. Your broker's 1099-B should list each lot separately, and if you sell after several vest dates you will need to identify which lots were sold, using specific identification rather than the default first-in-first-out.

What does the calculator do if I leave the company before vesting? Nothing, because unvested units are forfeited. This tool only models a vest that happened. Unvested RSUs have no value you can calculate or sell, which is also why job-change decisions rarely hinge on them once you account for the vesting schedule.

Does the 22 percent rate change in 2026? The supplemental wage rate has been fixed at 22 percent for years, with a 37 percent rate applying above $1 million in supplemental wages in a single year. California and several other states apply their own supplemental rates, commonly 10.23 percent in California, which is higher than many people expect.

Is the gain on shares sold at a loss deductible? Yes as a capital loss, and it can offset other capital gains plus up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year. If the price fell before vest and recovered by the time you sold, you may see a loss on paper at vest with no cash loss at all, which is why brokers often track a supplemental basis separately.
