EV Charging Cost Calculator

Electricity prices are printed on your bill, but the number that decides your monthly cost is cost per mile, and that depends on four things at once: your tariff, how efficiently the car converts power into distance, how much energy the charger loses, and how often you pay retail prices at a public station. This calculator combines all four and puts the result next to what the same miles would cost in a gasoline car.

How the EV Charging Cost Calculator Works

The chain from miles driven to dollars spent has three links, and most online estimates skip the middle one. Miles divided by efficiency gives the energy that has to reach the battery. That energy divided by charging efficiency gives the energy the meter actually bills. Multiply by the rate and you have cost.

Battery energy = monthly miles ÷ miles per kWh
Wall energy = battery energy ÷ charging efficiency
Monthly cost = wall energy × blended rate
Cost per mile = monthly cost ÷ monthly miles

Worked example with the default inputs. Driving 1,100 miles a month in a car that returns 3.5 miles per kWh needs 314 kWh at the battery. At 90 percent charging efficiency the wall draws 349 kWh. With 85 percent of that bought at home for 17 cents and 15 percent bought at a public charger for 43 cents, the blended rate works out to about 20.9 cents, so the month costs roughly $72.98. That is 6.6 cents per mile and about $876 a year.

The same 1,100 miles in a 30 MPG sedan at $3.30 a gallon burns 36.7 gallons and costs $121.00, or 11 cents a mile. The saving is about $48 a month — real, but far smaller than the marketing figures that compare home charging against a 20 MPG truck. Change the public share to 40 percent and the EV's monthly cost rises to roughly $95.70, cutting the advantage to $25. Most of the "EVs are nearly free to run" claims quietly assume 100 percent home charging at a cheap off-peak rate.

A full charge from empty is a separate, smaller number: 75 kWh divided by 0.90 is 83.3 kWh at the meter, times 17 cents, so about $14.17. Useful for a road-trip budget, less useful for a monthly one, because nobody arrives at a charger with a truly empty battery.

Why the Estimate Usually Comes Out Wrong

Using EPA efficiency as real-world efficiency. The EPA figure is a lab cycle. Highway speeds above 70 mph, winter heating and aggressive acceleration all push consumption up. A car rated 3.5 miles per kWh will often return 2.9 in January. Enter what the trip computer shows, not the window sticker.

Forgetting charging losses. Ten percent sounds trivial until you notice it is the same order of magnitude as the entire difference between a good and a bad electricity tariff. A page that multiplies battery capacity by rate silently understates every number it prints.

Treating the public rate as a single number. The 15 percent of charging done away from home can carry 40 percent of the cost, because public power is two to three times the residential price. The blended rate, not the home rate, is what you should be quoting to a spouse who is sceptical about the switch.

Ignoring the fixed costs. A home charger, an electrician, a permit and any panel upgrade are real money. They do not change cost per mile, but they do change whether the first two years save anything at all. Divide the install cost by expected lifetime miles if you want the honest comparison.

One more limitation worth stating plainly: this tool prices energy, not depreciation, insurance or tire wear. EVs tend to be heavier, and their tires often wear faster than a comparable gasoline car, a cost that can quietly absorb a chunk of the fuel saving.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to charge an EV from empty to full?

At the US average residential rate of about 17 cents per kWh and 90 percent charging efficiency, a 75 kWh battery costs roughly $14. A 100 kWh truck battery lands near $19, and a 40 kWh compact near $7.60. Off-peak plans cut those figures by 30 to 50 percent.

What is a normal cost per mile for an electric car?

Between 3 and 8 cents for home charging in most of the country, and 12 to 16 cents if you depend on DC fast chargers. Gasoline at $3.30 a gallon in a 30 MPG car is about 11 cents, which is the benchmark worth beating.

Does charging slower really save money?

Per kilowatt-hour, yes, slightly. Level 1 charging is around 85 percent efficient against roughly 90 to 92 percent for Level 2, so the same battery fill draws a few percent more energy from the wall. The bigger saving from slow charging is that it usually happens overnight on a cheaper tariff.

Why is my electricity bill higher than this estimate?

Three usual causes: a rate that includes delivery charges and taxes you did not add, a public-charging share higher than assumed, or winter consumption well below the rated miles per kWh. Compare a single month of billed kWh against the wall-energy figure to find which one it is.

Can I charge an EV entirely from home without a fast charger?

For most commuters, yes. A Level 1 outlet adds roughly 3 to 5 miles of range per hour, which covers 40 to 60 miles overnight. Anyone driving more than about 60 miles a day will want a 240 volt circuit, and the installation cost is the main reason to think about it before buying the car.

Do public charging networks charge per minute or per kWh?

Both exist. Per-kWh pricing is common where resale rules allow it, while per-minute pricing is used elsewhere and penalises cars that charge slowly or stay connected past 80 percent. Check the network's app before assuming a rate, since the same station can cost twice as much at peak hours.
