A down payment is the portion of a purchase paid upfront rather than financed. On a home it changes the loan amount, the monthly payment, and whether mortgage insurance applies; on a car it reduces both the financed balance and the interest charged on it.

For a $450,000 home, a 20% down payment is $90,000 and leaves a $360,000 loan. At 6.5% over 30 years that is about $2,275 a month. Drop to 10% — $45,000 down, $405,000 borrowed — and the payment rises to about $2,560, plus mortgage insurance of roughly $170 a month until equity reaches 20%. The smaller down payment costs around $455 a month more, which is the real price of preserving $45,000 in cash.

The decision is therefore not purely financial. Keeping cash for an emergency fund matters more than shaving a payment, and a home bought with a thin reserve is the scenario in which a single repair becomes a crisis. A common compromise is to put down enough to avoid insurance where possible, and never to drain the emergency fund to reach a round percentage.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much down payment do I need for a house?

Twenty percent avoids mortgage insurance on conventional loans, but many programs accept 3% to 5%. The right figure depends on how much cash reserve you keep afterwards.

What happens if I put down less than 20%?

Mortgage insurance is added, typically 0.3% to 1.5% of the loan annually, and it usually stays until equity reaches 20%.

Is a larger down payment always better?

Not necessarily. Draining savings to increase a down payment leaves no cushion for repairs or income disruption, which is a larger risk than a slightly higher payment.

Does a larger down payment reduce the interest rate?

Often yes. Lenders price risk, and a lower loan-to-value ratio frequently qualifies for a better rate.
