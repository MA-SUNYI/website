Siding Calculator

Siding is sold by the square — 100 square feet of wall — and the number of squares is the only figure a distributor will quote against. Getting there means measuring the perimeter, multiplying by wall height, adding the gable triangles, subtracting the openings, and then adding waste for the offcuts at every corner and frame. This calculator does that in one pass and returns panels, starter strip, J-channel and soffit alongside the squares, because the trim is what turns a material estimate into an order.

How the Siding Calculator Works

The wall is a rectangle wrapped around a footprint, so the first step is the perimeter: two times length plus two times width. Multiply that by wall height and you have the flat wall area, before any deductions. Gable ends are triangles on top of the rectangle, each equal to half the width times the rise, and dormers are small rectangles that should be added the same way.

The formula chain, in the order the calculator applies it:

Perimeter = 2 × (Length + Width)
Wall area = Perimeter × Wall height
Net area = Wall area + Gable area − Opening area
Squares = Net area × (1 + Waste) ÷ 100
Panel coverage = Panel length × Exposure ÷ 12
Panels = Squares (rounded up) × 100 ÷ Panel coverage

Worked example. A 40 by 30 foot single-storey house with 8 foot walls. Perimeter is 140 feet, wall area is 140 × 8 = 1,120 sq ft. Add 200 sq ft of gable and subtract 180 sq ft of windows and doors: net area 1,140 sq ft. At 10 percent waste that becomes 1,254 sq ft, or 12.54 squares, which you buy as 13 squares.

Now the panel count. A 12 foot panel at 4 inch exposure covers 12 × 4 ÷ 12 = 4 sq ft, so a square takes 25 panels. Thirteen squares is 325 panels. Switch to 6 inch exposure on the same panel length and each piece covers 6 sq ft, a square takes 16.7 panels, and the order drops to about 217 — a 108 panel difference on identical wall area. That is why exposure belongs in the input rather than buried in a footnote.

Trim runs on a different logic. Starter strip follows the bottom of every wall, so it equals the perimeter: 140 feet. J-channel frames each opening at roughly 20 linear feet per window or door, so 10 openings is about 200 feet, plus the eave line at another 140 feet. Soffit is the overhang underside: perimeter times overhang depth, 140 × 1 foot = 140 sq ft for a 12 inch overhang.

Where Siding Estimates Go Wrong

Treating it like paint. Paint estimators sometimes skip the deduction because a roller covers the frame edge anyway. Siding cannot bridge an opening, so windows and doors must come out. Skipping the deduction on a house with 10 openings overstates the order by roughly 15 percent.

Confusing exposure with board width. A "double-four" panel is not four inches wide — it is two four-inch courses on one panel, roughly eight inches of face. Exposure is the visible height after the lap, and it is the only figure that converts panels to squares correctly.

Ordering trim last. Starter strip, J-channel and corner posts are the parts that stop a job. They are cheap per foot and easy to underestimate, and a second trip for 40 feet of J-channel costs more in time than the trim itself.

Ignoring the gable and dormer. Gable ends are large triangles and often skipped because they are not part of the rectangle. On a 30 foot wide house with a 6 in 12 pitch, each gable is 30 × 7.5 ÷ 2 = 112 sq ft, and two of them are more than two squares.

Assuming one waste factor fits every house. A plain rectangle with four corners genuinely needs about 10 percent. Add a garage bump-out, a bay, three dormers and a wraparound porch, and 15 to 20 percent is the honest number. Under-ordering shows up as a visible colour mismatch on the reorder, because siding is dyed in lots.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many square feet is one square of siding?

One square is exactly 100 square feet of wall coverage, the unit every siding distributor prices in. A 1,200 square foot wall is 12 squares before waste. Bundles, boxes and crates are labelled against that unit, so converting to squares is the step that makes quotes comparable across brands.

Should I subtract windows and doors from a siding estimate?

Yes. Unlike paint, siding cannot bridge an opening, so the board stops at the frame and the piece cut out is usually too short to reuse. Subtract large openings such as windows, doors and garage doors, then keep 10 to 15 percent waste to cover cut-offs at corners, gables and frames.

What lap exposure should I use for vinyl siding?

Four inches is the standard double-four profile and the default in most vinyl estimates. Five and six inch exposures cover a wall with fewer courses but show more face, so they read as a wider board. Exposure changes the panel count, not the square footage, which is why the calculator asks for it separately.

How much starter strip and J-channel do I need?

Starter strip runs the full perimeter at the bottom of every wall, so it equals the perimeter in linear feet plus a little for corners. J-channel frames every window and door and runs along the soffit line — roughly 20 feet per opening plus the eave perimeter, with 10 percent added for mitres.

Does fiber cement need the same quantity as vinyl?

The square footage is identical because both cover the same wall, but the piece count is not. Fiber cement lap is commonly 8.25 inches wide with a 7 inch exposure in 12 foot lengths, so each plank covers about 7 square feet against roughly 4 for a 12 foot vinyl panel at 4 inch exposure.

How much waste should I add for a house with gables and dormers?

Ten percent suits a simple rectangular footprint with four corners and few openings. Fifteen percent is the safer default once you have gable ends, dormers, a garage bump-out or more than six openings, because every angle and frame produces an offcut that cannot be used elsewhere on the wall.
