Space Heater Cost Calculator

A plug-in space heater can add $40 to a winter electricity bill without anyone noticing, because the number on the box says 1,500 W and nothing about money. This calculator converts wattage, your kWh rate and the hours the element is really heating into cost per hour, per day and per month, then prices the same heat against a heat pump and a gas furnace. The duty cycle field is the one most people skip, and it is usually the difference between a frightening estimate and the bill that actually arrives.

How the Running Cost Is Calculated

Electricity is billed in kilowatt-hours, so the first step is dividing watts by 1,000. A 1,500 W heater is 1.5 kW, and one hour at full output uses 1.5 kWh. Everything else is multiplication, with one correction that matters more than the rest: the element is not on the whole time the switch is on.

Cost per hour at full power = (watts ÷ 1,000) × rate per kWh

Energy per day = (watts ÷ 1,000) × hours switched on × duty cycle

Cost per month = energy per day × rate per kWh × days

Worked example. A 1,500 W heater at 18.5 cents per kWh costs $0.2775 an hour at full power, which rounds to $0.28. Eight hours on the timer at a 60 percent duty cycle is 4.8 hours of actual heating, or 7.2 kWh a day. That is $1.33 a day, about $40 a month over 30 days, and roughly $200 across a 150 day heating season. Leave the same heater at full power around the clock instead and the month becomes 1,080 kWh, about $200 for one appliance.

Heat output follows directly from wattage: 1,500 W × 3.412 = 5,118 BTU per hour. Every resistance heater, whether ceramic, oil-filled, infrared or mica, turns electricity into heat at close to 100 percent at the point of use, so the BTU figure depends only on the watts. What differs between models is how that heat is delivered, and therefore how long the thermostat keeps the element on.

The 1,500 W ceiling is not arbitrary. At 120 volts that is 12.5 amps, and a standard household circuit is 15 amps. Why the Duty Cycle Changes Everything

A heater switched on for eight hours is rarely a heater drawing full power for eight hours. Once the room reaches the set temperature the thermostat opens, the element cools, and the unit waits. In a small, well-sealed bedroom that can mean the element runs 30 percent of the time. In a drafty living room with a cold wall it can run 90 percent of the time, and the difference between those two cases is more than $70 a month on the same heater.

You can measure the real number instead of guessing it. A plug-in power meter left on the heater for one evening gives you the kWh used; divide by the hours on the clock and you have the average draw. If an eight hour evening shows 7.2 kWh, the average is 900 W, or 60 percent of a 1,500 W unit. Enter that 60 in the duty cycle field. A heater with a mechanical thermostat and no timer is the worst case, because it keeps cycling all night in a room nobody is in.

Two more details swing the result. Switching from high to low usually halves the draw to 750 W, which cuts the hourly cost in half but also halves the heat, so it changes the bill only if the room still reaches temperature. And the fan, display and remote receiver draw 10 to 30 W continuously, which is trivial next to the element but never zero.

Cost per Million BTU: When a Space Heater Is the Cheap Option

Comparing fuels needs a common unit, and one million BTU is the usual one. Resistance heat needs 293 kWh to deliver a million BTU, because 1,000,000 ÷ 3,412 = 293. At 18.5 cents that is $54.22. A heat pump moving heat at a coefficient of performance of 3 needs only 97.7 kWh, or $18.07. A 95 percent efficient gas furnace burning $1.10 gas needs 10.53 therms, about $11.58.

Read that table honestly and the space heater looks like the most expensive way to make heat in the house, which it is per BTU. It still makes sense in one specific situation: when it lets you turn the central system down and heat only the room you are sitting in. A 1,500 W heater running four effective hours a day costs about $33 a month. If that replaces a furnace holding 2,000 square feet at 68 degrees, the saving is real. If the furnace stays at 68 and the heater runs as well, you have simply bought the same heat twice, at the higher price.

Zone heating also has a floor. Rooms with plumbing should not be allowed to drop near freezing, and a heat pump or furnace running at a low setpoint is often cheaper than several space heaters left on in separate rooms. The break-even depends on how many rooms you actually occupy at once, which is a household fact, not a formula.

What This Calculator Leaves Out

The rate field is the energy charge only. Delivery charges, riders, taxes and monthly service fees can add 20 to 40 percent to the effective price of a kWh, so enter the all-in figure from your bill if you want the total to match. Time-of-use plans matter just as much: heating at 40 cents in an evening peak costs more than twice as much as the same heat at 18 cents overnight, and a heater is exactly the kind of load that lands in the peak.

Sizing is the other gap. The common rule is 10 W per square foot of floor area, which puts a 1,500 W heater at about 150 square feet. A larger room, a vaulted ceiling, single-pane windows or an uninsulated wall all push the duty cycle toward 100 percent, so the heater runs flat out and still feels weak. In that case the estimate here is accurate and the heater is simply the wrong size.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a 1,500 watt space heater really produce 5,118 BTU?

Yes. One watt of resistance heat equals 3.412 BTU per hour, so 1,500 watts is 5,118 BTU per hour at full output. Ceramic, oil-filled and infrared models all land on the same figure for the same wattage, because each converts electricity to heat at close to 100 percent.

Why is my real bill lower than the full-power estimate?

Because the element is not on the whole time. A thermostat cuts it once the room reaches the set temperature and restarts it as the room cools, so eight hours on the clock is often four or five hours of draw. Enter that lower number as the duty cycle and the estimate lines up with the bill.

Can a 1,500 watt heater share a 15 amp circuit?

Only with care. At 120 volts it pulls 12.5 amps, 83 percent of a 15 amp breaker, which is above the 80 percent continuous-load guideline. Plug it into a wall outlet rather than a power strip and keep it off any circuit already carrying a hair dryer, microwave or another heater.

Do oil-filled or infrared heaters cost less to run than ceramic ones?

Not at the same wattage. The bill follows kilowatts, not the label, so two 1,500 W heaters cost the same per hour of full output. Oil-filled units keep releasing heat after the element stops and infrared warms surfaces rather than air, which changes comfort and cycle length, not the price per kWh.

Is heating one room cheaper than running the furnace?

Only if you actually lower the central thermostat. Per million BTU, resistance heat is about $54, a heat pump at COP 3 about $18, and a 95 percent gas furnace about $12 at $1.10 a therm. The heater wins when it replaces whole-house heating for one room, and loses when the furnace keeps running.

How large a room will a 1,500 watt heater keep warm?

Roughly 150 square feet in a normally insulated room, using the 10 watts per square foot rule. Drafty windows, an uninsulated wall or an open stairwell can push the same unit to a 100 percent duty cycle, so the room stays cool while the monthly cost roughly doubles.
