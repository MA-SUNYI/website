Podcast Sponsorship Rate Calculator

Most podcasters who underprice their show do not lack an audience. They lack a number they can defend in a reply email. This calculator takes the only figures a sponsor actually asks about, which are 30-day downloads, niche and placement, and turns them into a rate card: a CPM band, a per-episode quote to open with, a target, and a private floor that tells you when to walk. It is built for the negotiation, not the forecast.

How the sponsorship rate is built

Podcast advertising is priced the same way it has been priced for twenty years: a CPM, which is the cost an advertiser pays for one thousand downloads, multiplied against the downloads you can prove. Every honest rate card on the market is that multiplication plus two adjustments, one for how valuable the audience is to advertisers and one for where in the episode the read sits.

Base CPM is $22 for a US, general-interest, host-read mid-roll. Niche multiplier runs from 0.85 for a hobby show to 1.35 for finance or B2B, because a financial services brand will pay several times what a snack brand pays to reach the same thousand people. Placement multiplier is 1.4 for a mid-roll, 1.0 for a pre-roll and 0.65 for a post-roll, because listener retention falls off a cliff after the final segment. Country multiplier is 0.6 for a UK, Canada or Australia-heavy audience and 0.35 for a mixed or non-English one.

Effective CPM = 22 x niche x placement x country
Rate per spot = downloads / 1,000 x effective CPM
Monthly revenue = rate per spot x slots per episode x episodes x fill rate
Floor per spot = rate per spot x 0.7

Worked example: 6,000 downloads, a tech and business show, one host-read mid-roll, a mostly US audience. The effective CPM is 22 x 1.15 x 1.4 x 1.0 = $35.42. Downloads divided by a thousand is 6, so one spot is worth $212.52. Sell two spots in each of four episodes a month at a 70 percent fill rate and monthly revenue is 212.52 x 2 x 4 x 0.7 = $1,190.11. The group floor of three conversation numbers works out to an anchor of $255.02, a target of $212.52 and a private floor of $148.77.

Why the download number you quote decides the whole negotiation

The single most common reason a small show gets talked down is a download figure that cannot be audited. All-time downloads, follower counts and a "we reach about 20,000 people a month" estimate all sound bigger than a 30-day per-episode number, and every experienced buyer knows it. The moment one of your figures does not reconcile with what they see in a measurement panel, everything else in the conversation gets discounted by a third, and the discount happens silently.

The 30-day window is the defensible one because of how podcast consumption actually works. Episode files are downloaded by the listening app, usually once, and left on the phone. Downloads recorded in the first four weeks after publication are a close proxy for the real audience of that episode, while downloads in month nine are often devices re-syncing a queue. Quoting the 30-day figure makes your rate card the one number in the room that nobody can challenge, and it lets you ask for the mid-roll CPM rather than apologising for a pre-roll one.

Two related mistakes are worth avoiding. First, do not use a niche multiplier you cannot demonstrate. Claiming a finance-level CPM on a show whose audience is mostly casual listeners will get you one deal and no renewal, because the sponsor will measure the result and find it lacking. Second, do not sell every slot. A host-read ad in every break is the fastest way to teach listeners to skip forward, and skipping is precisely the behaviour that makes your inventory hard to price next quarter.

One honest limitation: this calculator has no idea what your audience retention curve actually looks like. A mid-roll placed at the fifteen-minute mark of an average listen time of twelve minutes is not really a mid-roll. Pull your own completion rate from the hosting platform before applying the 1.4 multiplier, and if the read sits after most listeners drop off, price it as a post-roll.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I charge for a 60-second host-read spot in the middle of an episode?

Price it as downloads divided by 1,000, multiplied by a mid-roll CPM. A general-interest show commanding a $22 CPM with 6,000 downloads per episode lands at $132 per spot. Finance, B2B and software shows routinely defend $45 to $70 CPMs on the same download count, so the niche multiplier matters more than the number on your dashboard.

Why do buyers ask for a 30-day download number instead of all-time downloads?

Because most podcast apps download an episode once and never refresh the count, the first four weeks capture nearly all the real audience. A 90-day or all-time figure flatters a show whose back catalog is still accumulating downloads. Quoting the 30-day number is what makes your rate card survive a buyer who checks it against a third-party measurement panel.

Is it better to sell six slots a month at a low CPM or three at a high one?

Usually the second. Each additional ad read costs the show a listener minute and a little trust, and buyers rarely reward volume with a better rate. Fewer, well-priced reads in categories that fit the show also make renewal easier, because the sponsor measures a clean result instead of competing with five other reads in the same episode.

Do I need to discount for a multi-episode package?

A 5 to 15 percent package discount is normal and worth giving, but only in exchange for commitment. Ten episodes booked in advance is worth more than a 20 percent premium you might negotiate episode by episode, because it fills inventory, reduces selling time and gives the sponsor a fair test period. Never give a discount for reach you do not have yet.

How is a programmatic or dynamically inserted spot priced differently from a host-read?

Programmatic spots are bought without a conversation and filled by an ad server, so they clear at roughly a third to a half of what a host-read earns. The trade is effort against rate. Programmatic fills leftover inventory at almost no cost to you; host-reads take recording time and script work but pay per spot several times over.

What should the floor be when a sponsor pushes back on my rate?

Set it before the conversation, at about 70 percent of your target. Below that number, a slot is better left unsold or offered to a smaller, better-fitting brand, because a cheap read still consumes the listener minute you could have sold at full price next quarter. The floor is a private number, never a number you announce.
