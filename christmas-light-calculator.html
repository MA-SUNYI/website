Christmas Light Calculator

Decorating goes wrong in two directions. Buy too few strands and the roofline ends two feet short of the gable, which means a second trip in December traffic. Buy too many and you are storing a box of unopened lights for eleven months, or worse, overloading a circuit that was already carrying a freezer. This calculator takes the linear feet you want to cover, the spacing between bulbs and the strand you plan to buy, and returns the strand count, the bulb count, the total wattage, the amps drawn and how many strands one circuit will carry.

How the Count Is Worked Out

Three numbers decide everything: how many feet you are covering, how far apart the bulbs sit, and how many bulbs come on one strand. Bulb spacing converts feet into bulbs, bulbs per strand converts bulbs into strands, and the strand length is whatever the factory put between the bulbs, which is why a 100 bulb mini strand at 4 inch spacing measures about 33 feet while a 25 bulb C9 strand at 12 inch spacing measures 25 feet.

Bulbs per foot = 12 ÷ spacing in inches

Strand length in feet = bulbs per strand × spacing ÷ 12

Strands needed = linear feet × (1 + allowance) ÷ strand length, rounded up

Watts = bulbs × watts per bulb, and amps = watts ÷ 120

Worked example. An 80 foot run at 12 inch spacing with 25 bulb C9 LED strands. Each strand is 25 × 12 ÷ 12 = 25 feet long. With a 10 percent allowance for peaks and clips, 80 × 1.1 = 88 feet, and 88 ÷ 25 = 3.52, rounded up to 4 strands. Those 4 strands carry 100 bulbs at 0.8 W, so 80 W total and 0.67 amps at 120 volts. A 15 amp circuit holds that comfortably, which matters because the same 100 bulbs in incandescent C9 at 5 W would draw 500 W and 4.17 amps.

Run the same 80 feet in mini lights at 4 inch spacing and the math flips. A 100 bulb strand covers 100 × 4 ÷ 12 = 33.3 feet, so 88 feet needs three strands, 300 bulbs, and at 0.34 W that is 102 W, or 0.85 amps. Dense spacing multiplies bulb count by three and the wattage barely moves, which is the whole argument for LED mini lights.

Spacing and Strand Reference

Spacing is a style decision with an arithmetic consequence. Twelve inches reads as classic C9, nine inches is a fuller traditional look, six inches looks dense, and four inches is the mini light look most people picture when they think of a lit tree or a bushy hedge.

Watts, Amps and How Many Strands Fit a Circuit

A circuit is not a suggestion. A 15 amp breaker at 120 volts supplies 1,800 watts in theory, but continuous loads are meant to stay at 80 percent of the rating, so treat 1,440 watts as the working ceiling, or 1,920 watts on a 20 amp circuit. The table below is what that ceiling means in strands.

C9 LED, 0.8 W80 W0.67 A72 strands of 25

The gap between the first and last row is the reason a house full of old C9 bulbs needs three circuits where the same display in LED needs one. It is also why the "max connect" figure printed on a strand is worth reading twice. That limit usually protects a small fuse inside the male plug and the wire gauge of the cord, not the house breaker, so a strand may stop you at three connections long before the circuit is anywhere near full. Both limits apply at once, and the stricter one wins.

Common Usage

  • Measuring a roofline along the eave and up both rakes, then adding 10 percent for peaks and clips.
  • Sizing a shrub run: wrapping a bush loosely takes roughly three times its height in linear feet, dense wrapping about five.
  • Checking whether the front yard display can share the porch outlet with a chest freezer or a sump pump.
  • Deciding between incandescent C9 and LED C9 before buying 20 strands of the wrong one.

Where People Get the Numbers Wrong

The most common error is measuring the footprint of the roof rather than the slope where the lights hang. A roof section that runs 12 feet horizontally and rises 9 feet has 15 feet of rake, because 144 plus 81 is 225 and the square root is 15. Measure that from the plan and you are 3 feet short, and a matching pair of rakes is 6 feet short, which is a whole strand at 12 inch spacing.

The second error is trusting a wattage figure from memory. A box marked "C9" can be 5 W incandescent or 0.8 W LED, and the difference is a factor of six in total load. The same applies to mini lights: 0.41 W incandescent against 0.34 W LED looks trivial per bulb, but across 1,200 bulbs it separates a comfortable single circuit from a tripped breaker.

The third is daisy-chaining past the printed limit because the lights still work. They will work right up to the point where the fuse in the plug opens, usually on the coldest evening of the year, and the whole run goes dark with no obvious cause. Keep inline connections at or below the manufacturer limit, use a grounded outdoor cord rated for the load, and put the whole thing on a GFCI outlet. Wet snow and a non-GFCI circuit is the one failure in this list that is genuinely dangerous rather than merely annoying.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many lights do I need for 100 feet of roofline at 12 inch spacing?

One hundred bulbs, since 12 inch spacing is one bulb per foot. In C9 strands of 25 that is four strands, and the calculator adds the waste allowance on top so the last run is not two feet short at the gable.

Why does my strand say no more than three connected end to end?

The limit is the fuse in the male plug and the wire inside the strand, not the house breaker. Three 25 bulb incandescent C9 strands draw 375 W, about 3.1 A, which is already near what that small fuse tolerates. A fourth strand pushes it, and the fuse is doing its job when it opens.

Do LED strands let me connect more lights?

Far more on the circuit: about 72 C9 LED strands against 11 incandescent ones on a 15 A breaker. The printed connect limit still applies, because it protects the cord rather than the breaker, so read it separately from the circuit math.

Should I measure the roof footprint or the slope?

The slope, measured along the eave or rake where the lights actually hang. A section that runs 12 feet out and rises 9 feet is 15 feet of rake rather than 12, so a plan view leaves you short at the peak.

How many strands can go on one outdoor outlet?

Add the watts of every strand on that outlet and keep the total under 1,440 W on a 15 A circuit. That is 11 incandescent C9 strands of 25 bulbs or 42 mini LED strands of 100. If the outlet also feeds a freezer, subtract that load first.

What gauge extension cord do I need?

16 gauge suits about 10 A up to 25 feet, 14 gauge up to 50 feet, and 12 gauge up to 100 feet. Long thin cords drop voltage, dim the far end of the run and warm the cord, so match the gauge to the distance as well as the load.
