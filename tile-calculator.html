Tile Calculator

Tile is priced by the square foot, sold by the box, and glued down with two materials almost nobody counts before the first trip to the store. This calculator returns the tile count, the boxes to order, the grout weight in pounds and the thinset bags for one flat surface. It uses the joint-inclusive tile footprint that manufacturers build their coverage charts on, so the answer lines up with the label on the carton instead of drifting a box or two short.

Result:

Planning estimate for one rectangular surface. Subtract cabinets, islands or tub surrounds before entering your dimensions. Mosaic sheets should be entered at sheet size (usually 12 x 12 in), not chip size.

How the numbers are produced

Three quantities come out of one chain of arithmetic. First the area, then the tile footprint including its share of the grout joint, then the waste allowance on top:

Tile footprint (sq ft) = (tile length + joint) × (tile width + joint) ÷ 144

Tiles to lay = surface area ÷ tile footprint, rounded up

Tiles to buy = tiles to lay × (1 + waste factor), rounded up

The joint appears in both dimensions because every tile owns half a joint on each of its four edges. A 12 inch tile with an eighth inch joint therefore covers 12.125 inches, or 1.021 square feet — not the 1.00 square foot most people assume when they divide 120 by 1.

Worked example: a 12 by 10 foot floor is 120 square feet. With 12 by 12 inch tile, a 1/8 inch joint and 10 percent waste, the footprint is 12.125 × 12.125 ÷ 144 = 1.021 sq ft. That gives 117.5 tiles laid, so 118, and 130 tiles to buy. At 15 square feet per box that is 9 boxes, or 135 tiles — five spare, which is the correct amount of spare.

Grout is not a percentage of the floor. It is the volume of the gaps between tiles:

Joint length per sq ft (in) = 144 × (tile length + tile width) ÷ (tile length × tile width)

Grout (lb) = joint length × joint width × joint depth × 0.0578 × area × 1.1

For the 12 inch tile above, that is 24 inches of joint per square foot, so 24 × 0.125 × 0.375 = 1.125 cubic inches per square foot. At about 0.0578 pounds per cubic inch that is 0.065 pounds per square foot, or 8.6 pounds for the 120 square foot floor with a 10 percent cushion. One 10 pound box covers it. Thinset is simpler still: area divided by the coverage printed for your trowel notch, rounded up to whole 50 pound bags.

The cost of being short is not the tile. It is the second trip, the discontinued shade lot, and a floor with a visible colour seam where the new boxes meet the old. Over-ordering by one box costs perhaps 40 dollars; under-ordering by one box on a 300 square foot floor can mean re-tiling a whole room in a different dye lot. Keep the leftovers dry and they become the repair stock for the next ten years.

Grout scales with joint length, and small tile punishes you

The same 120 square foot floor needs about 8.6 pounds of grout in 12 inch tile and roughly 100 pounds in 1 inch mosaic, because the mosaic has twelve times the joint line per square foot. This is the single most common material shortfall on shower projects, where mosaic is standard and the grout is bought by feel.

Joint depth is the tile thickness, so thicker stone means more grout per foot of joint, not less. A 3/8 inch saltillo at 3/4 inch thick needs double the grout of a 3/8 inch porcelain at the same joint width. Sealed stone also absorbs more grout into the edges than glazed porcelain, which is why stone installers habitually add another 10 percent.

Matching the trowel to the tile

Thinset coverage is set by the notch, not the tile size, and the notch is set by the tile. A 1/4 inch square notch spreads about 85 square feet per 50 pound bag and is right for tile up to about 12 inches. Above 15 inches the standard practice is a 1/2 inch square notch at roughly 40 square feet per bag, plus back-buttering, which adds close to 18 percent. This calculator applies that back-buttering surcharge automatically once either tile dimension reaches 15 inches, because large-format tile set with a small notch will sound hollow within a year.

Coverage figures on the bag assume a flat, true substrate. A wavy floor consumes noticeably more mortar as the notch is flattened out to meet the tile, so add a bag on any slab you have not checked with a straightedge.

What this calculator does not know

It assumes one flat rectangular surface. An L-shaped room has to be split into two rectangles and run twice, and a shower has three walls that each need their own measurement unless you subtract the door opening. It does not subtract a tub, a vanity or a kitchen island, and those omissions are usually larger than the waste factor.

It also says nothing about whether the substrate will hold tile. Deflection, waterproofing in wet areas, crack isolation membrane and expansion joints are the parts of the job that fail, and none of them appear on a material list. A perfectly calculated tile order over a floor that flexes is still a failed floor.

Finally, layout is not arithmetic. Where the first course starts decides how large the cut at each wall will be, and a room that is 11 feet 10 inches wide will produce a 1 inch sliver on one side if you centre a 12 inch grid carelessly. Dry-lay two courses before mixing thinset.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should the grout joint be added to the tile size?

Yes. Each tile takes half a joint on all four sides, so a 12 inch tile with a 1/8 inch joint occupies 12.125 inches. Skipping the joint overstates the tile count by about 2 percent on large tile and closer to 5 percent on subway tile, which is most of a box on a typical wall.

Why does 1 inch mosaic need twelve times the grout of 12 inch tile?

Because grout fills joint length, not floor area. One square foot of 12 inch tile has 24 inches of joint line; one square foot of 1 inch mosaic has 288 inches. Same floor, same joint width, twelve times the grout by weight.

How many extra tiles should I keep after the job?

Two to three percent of the order, and always from the same shade lot as the installed tile. They are not for the next project — they are for the cracked tile in year four, when the line may no longer be manufactured in that colour.

Can I use this for a shower with three walls?

Run it three times, once per wall, and subtract the door opening from the wall that contains it. Shower floors are a separate run with a different tile and a slope, and the drain cutout should be ignored rather than subtracted.

Does back-buttering really change the thinset quantity?

It adds roughly 18 percent, and it is not optional above about 15 inches. The trowel lines alone leave voids under a large tile; back-buttering fills them. The calculator applies the surcharge automatically at that size.

Is it cheaper to buy tile by the box or by the square foot?

Boxes are the only real unit at retail, so the square-foot price is a derived figure. Two boxes of 12 by 24 at 8 tiles each cover 32 square feet; the same money in 12 by 12 may cover 30 or 34 depending on the carton. Divide the box price by the coverage printed on the label before comparing anything.
